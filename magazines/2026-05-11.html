Promoting Psychological Wellbeing: The Role of Digital Political Participation and Network Diversity
Astuti S.W., Rina N., Putra D.K.S., Mukhlisiana L.

Digital political participation on social media functions as a catalyst for psychological empowerment among Generation Z voters, according to a new quantitative study published in Frontiers in Political Science. Surveying young citizens, the researchers found that engaging in online political activities — sharing news, commenting on policy debates, signing digital petitions — enhances individuals' sense of agency and political efficacy. Crucially, the relationship is mediated by network diversity: participants who interacted across ideologically varied online communities reported significantly higher eudaimonic wellbeing than those who remained in homogeneous information environments. The finding complicates the dominant narrative that social-media politics is inherently toxic to mental health, suggesting that the quality of the network matters more than participation itself.

Practitioner implication: Campaign digital teams should design supporter communities that expose participants to varied perspectives rather than reinforcing echo chambers — the psychological payoff for volunteers may improve retention and sustained engagement.
Frontiers in Political Science, 2026 · doi: 10.3389/fpos.2026.1790670

Election Deniers Could Become Governors — and Certify 2028

In Arizona, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin — three states that decided the 2020 presidential election — Republicans who actively tried to overturn those results are now frontrunners for the 2026 governor's race. Rep. Andy Biggs, who voted against certifying Biden's victory and joined a friend-of-the-court brief seeking to void results in four states, is the overwhelming favorite for the GOP nomination in Arizona. In Wisconsin, Rep. Tom Tiffany leads the Republican field. In Pennsylvania, state Treasurer Stacy Garrity has consolidated support. Across 23 states, including five presidential swing states, candidates who have denied election results are running for offices that will have a direct role in certifying future elections. The stakes extend far beyond 2026: governors in these battleground states hold primary authority over the administration and certification of presidential elections. A sweep by election-denying candidates would place Trump allies in charge of the very machinery they sought to disrupt six years ago — just in time for 2028.

AI Infrastructure Becomes the New Third Rail of American Politics

Local opposition to AI data centers has exploded into a bipartisan electoral force. In Pennsylvania alone, four competitive House districts sit inside Governor Josh Shapiro's data-center expansion zone — all held by Republicans now rated toss-ups by the Cook Political Report. Electric bills in the state rose 21.7 percent in 2025, and Democratic challengers have seized on the connection between AI infrastructure and rising costs. Shapiro has since dialed back his enthusiasm, proposing guidelines that require companies to fund their own energy infrastructure. The backlash is not confined to swing states: opponents delayed or blocked 48 data-center projects nationwide in 2025, threatening $156 billion in planned investment. What was once a fringe NIMBY concern has become a central campaign issue uniting environmentalists, farmers worried about lost farmland, and conservative voters furious about rising utility bills.

Colombia: Cepeda Consolidates at 44% as Right Fragments Before May 31

Ongoing: Colombian presidential race — fresh polling update

With twenty days until Colombia's presidential first round, left-wing senator Iván Cepeda has strengthened his commanding lead. The latest Invamer poll, published April 26, puts Cepeda at 44.3 percent of voting intention — a seven-point jump from February — while the right-wing vote remains split between two contenders. Abelardo De la Espriella holds 21.5 percent; Paloma Valencia, the fastest-rising candidate in the race, has nearly doubled to 19.8 percent. Former frontrunners Claudia López and Sergio Fajardo have collapsed to single digits, their centrist coalitions hollowed out by Cepeda's expansion across age groups and regions.

The structural dynamic now favors Cepeda heavily. Even if the right consolidates behind a single candidate, the combined De la Espriella-Valencia vote trails Cepeda by three points, and Colombian electoral law restricts publication of new polls after May 24. If no candidate secures 50 percent plus one, a runoff between the two top finishers will be held on June 21. For the international campaign community, Colombia offers a case study in how a disciplined left-wing candidacy can win by expanding its coalition rather than simply mobilizing the base — a lesson with echoes for progressive campaigns from Brazil to France.

Code for America and Anthropic Launch AI Benefits Navigator for SNAP Caseworkers

Announced on the main stage of the 2026 Code for America Summit in Chicago, a new partnership between the civic-tech nonprofit and Anthropic will deploy Claude-based tools to help government caseworkers administer public benefits — starting with the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). The first tool, called the SNAP Policy Navigator, gives caseworkers real-time access to federal, state, and county SNAP guidance, reducing administrative burdens tied to eligibility determinations and policy interpretation. The integration focuses on three core tasks: answering complex policy questions, reviewing eligibility documents, and drafting plain-language communications to benefit recipients. The partnership is designed to produce reusable frameworks that can be adapted across states and program areas — a significant step toward AI-augmented social services at scale. The announcement follows Anthropic's separate $20 million donation to Public First Action, a pro-regulation AI advocacy group, signaling the company's two-track strategy of civic deployment and policy engagement.

WEF GovTech Compass: Ten Principles for AI-Era Government

The World Economic Forum's Global Future Council on GovTech and Digital Public Infrastructure has published its GovTech Compass — a ten-principle framework for governments navigating AI integration into public services. Released on May 6, the compass is structured around three themes that determine whether digital reform creates meaningful public outcomes: legitimacy, trust, and delivery. The framework argues that governments now face a challenge beyond simple digitization: ensuring that technological innovation strengthens, rather than weakens, democratic legitimacy and public accountability. The compass is deliberately not a rulebook but a navigation tool for trade-offs — when to prioritize speed over inclusivity, when to centralize data infrastructure versus federating it, and how to manage the tension between AI-driven efficiency and human oversight. The publication arrives as the EU's Apply AI Summit (November 17 in Brussels) takes shape and the RAISE Summit in Paris (July 8-9) prepares to convene nine thousand AI leaders, making 2026 a watershed year for European GovTech policymaking.

Florida Signs Data-Center Law: States Draw the Line on AI Infrastructure

Governor Ron DeSantis signed SB 484 on May 7, making Florida the latest state to impose regulations on AI data-center expansion. The law requires the Public Service Commission to develop tariffs ensuring that large-load customers bear their full cost of service, preventing cost-shifting onto residential ratepayers. It also reinforces local governments' authority to refuse data-center construction in their jurisdictions. The bill passed the state Senate in the final hours of the 2026 session, rewritten by the House following conversations between the pro-AI White House and state legislators — a rare instance of intra-Republican tension between federal tech-boosterism and state consumer protection. DeSantis had championed stricter regulations than the final bill delivered, but praised the Legislature for acting. Combined with the Pennsylvania backlash, the Florida law signals an emerging bipartisan consensus: AI infrastructure must pay its own way. The political question for 2026 is whether data-center regulation becomes a winning campaign issue beyond swing states.

DOJ Sues 23 States for Voter Rolls: The Surveillance Risk to Civic Participation

The Trump administration's campaign to seize state voter registration records has escalated into a nationwide legal battle. Attorney General Pam Bondi has requested complete voter rolls from multiple states, including Social Security numbers and driver's license data. As of this month, the DOJ has filed lawsuits against at least 23 states and the District of Columbia that have refused to comply. The ostensible purpose — detecting noncitizen voting — relies on federal databases with well-documented historical inaccuracies. But the deeper concern, articulated by Higher Ground Labs and democracy-tech advocates, is that centralized federal access to voter rolls could enable surveillance tied to political behavior: cross-referencing individuals across databases, monitoring civic participation, and identifying people for targeted enforcement or intimidation. The crisis has prompted a rethinking of political-tech infrastructure: data sovereignty, encryption, and distributed architectures are now considered baseline requirements for operating in an environment of active government pressure on voter data.

NBC News reports that AI-funded ads are flooding the 2026 election cycle — but notably, most of them are about issues other than AI itself, suggesting the industry is buying broad political influence rather than narrowly defending its regulatory interests. The AAPCA survey finding that 64 percent of Republican consultants use AI daily, compared to 49 percent of Democrats, adds a partisan layer to the spending war.

Should AI Companies Fund Political Campaigns?

The Case for Strict Limits

AI companies wield outsized economic and informational power. When they pour $140 million into super PACs, they risk capturing the regulatory process before voters even understand what is being regulated. Senator Elizabeth Warren and progressive advocacy groups argue that AI campaign spending is structurally different from traditional corporate lobbying because the same companies building tools to influence elections are simultaneously funding the candidates who will regulate those tools. Transparency alone is insufficient; meaningful contribution limits and recusal requirements are necessary to prevent a feedback loop between AI-generated political content and AI-funded political campaigns.

Framing drawn from: CNBC, NBC News, American Prospect, progressive policy outlets

The Case for Participation

AI companies, like all American businesses, have a First Amendment right to participate in political discourse. The alternative — restricting an industry from engaging in the democratic process at the precise moment it is being regulated — would set a dangerous precedent. Leading the Future and allied groups argue that a unified national framework, achieved through political engagement, is far preferable to a chaotic patchwork of fifty state laws that would slow innovation and push AI development offshore. Venture capitalists and tech executives contend that the real risk is not too much industry participation but too little: without industry input, regulations will be drafted by people who do not understand the technology.

Framing drawn from: Quartz, Daily Signal, venture capital commentary, free-speech advocacy

Spread 14 — Political Tech Application
Quiller AI → Grassroots Analytics

What it does: Quiller is an AI-powered fundraising co-pilot that generates first-draft fundraising emails, SMS messages, press releases, and digital ad copy in under 20 seconds. The platform was trained on thousands of hand-tagged pieces of Democratic and progressive content, learning the stylistic and tonal patterns that drive donor engagement. Each organization further trains a private workspace on its own talking points and writing samples.


Who is using it: Originally built by Democratic strategist Mike Nellis (founder of Authentic Campaigns), Quiller was acquired in August 2025 by Grassroots Analytics, a D.C.-based political data firm. The acquisition merged Quiller's content-generation engine with Grassroots Analytics' donor intelligence platform, creating an end-to-end system that identifies high-potential donors and then generates personalized outreach at scale. The combined platform is now deployed across multiple Democratic congressional campaigns and allied organizations in the 2026 cycle.


What outcome has been reported: Quiller claims to produce copy that is "70 percent of the way done," reducing the time campaigns spend on fundraising emails from hours to minutes. Grassroots Analytics reports that the integrated platform has increased email open rates and donor response rates, though specific metrics remain proprietary.


> takeaway: The Quiller-Grassroots merger exemplifies the 2026 trend toward vertical integration in political tech — combining AI content generation with data analytics to close the loop between identifying supporters and activating them. The acquisition also highlights the consolidation wave in progressive tech, where Higher Ground Labs-backed startups are being absorbed into larger platforms.
