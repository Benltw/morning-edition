Research Insights of the Day

Political Misinformation Across Countries and Platforms

Humprecht, E., Valenzuela, S., Esser, F. & Tandoc, E.C. · The International Journal of Press/Politics · Special Issue Introduction · February 2026

Despite a decade of research, most of what scholars know about political misinformation comes from single-country, single-platform studies — overwhelmingly focused on the United States and Facebook or Twitter/X. This special issue introduction argues that the field’s findings cannot be generalized without systematic cross-national and cross-platform comparisons. The authors review emerging evidence showing that misinformation exposure, belief, and sharing behavior vary dramatically by media system type, platform architecture, and political polarization levels. Countries with strong public service broadcasting and lower polarization tend to show greater resilience to misinformation, while platform-specific affordances like algorithmic amplification and ephemeral content create distinct misinformation ecologies that single-platform studies miss entirely. The special issue brings together studies spanning multiple continents and platforms to begin closing this gap.

Practitioner implication: Campaign communicators operating across borders — or advising international clients — cannot assume that what works to counter misinformation in the US will translate elsewhere. Platform choice, media system context, and national polarization levels all mediate effectiveness, making locally calibrated strategies essential.

Source: SAGE Journals · The International Journal of Press/Politics · 2026

The One-Page Deal: US and Iran Close In on a Ceasefire Memo

The contours of a potential end to the Iran war are emerging in a single-page memorandum of understanding being negotiated through Pakistani intermediaries. According to Axios and CNN, the MOU would declare an end to hostilities and open a 30-day negotiation window covering three interlocking issues: reopening the Strait of Hormuz, limiting Iran’s nuclear programme, and lifting US sanctions. The enrichment moratorium is the central sticking point — Iran has proposed five years, Washington demands twenty, and three sources told CNBC that twelve to fifteen years is the likely landing zone. Tehran would also ship its stockpile of highly enriched uranium out of the country. Iran’s Foreign Ministry said the US proposal remains under review, with a formal response expected Thursday via Pakistan. Trump sharpened his rhetoric Tuesday, warning that bombing would resume at a higher intensity if Iran does not agree. Meanwhile, China’s foreign minister met with Iran’s Abbas Araghchi in Beijing, calling for a comprehensive ceasefire. Israel, which bombed Beirut’s southern suburbs the same day, is reportedly pressing the White House to resist last-minute concessions. For campaign strategists, the timeline matters enormously: a deal before summer would give Trump a foreign-policy victory narrative heading into the midterm stretch; a collapse would hand Democrats the chaos argument they have been sharpening for months.

Sources: Axios · CNN · CNBC · Al Jazeera

Britain Votes: Reform UK Eyes 1,300 Seats in a Once-in-a-Generation Realignment

Polls open across England, Scotland, and Wales today in the biggest set of UK elections outside a general election. Over 5,000 English council seats are being contested, alongside the Scottish Parliament and the newly expanded 96-seat Welsh Senedd. The projected headline: Reform UK is forecast to gain more than 1,300 council seats from a base of just three, potentially taking control of county councils in Essex, Norfolk, and Suffolk — a historic realignment in England’s rural heartlands. Labour is bracing for catastrophe: Oxford professor Stephen Fisher projects the party will lose 1,900 councillors, with flagship councils including Sunderland, Barnsley, and Wakefield flipping to Reform or no overall control. YouGov’s final national poll puts Reform at 25 percent, Labour at 18 percent, and the Conservatives at 17 percent — a three-way squeeze that has no modern precedent. The Greens are also surging, projected to gain 555 seats. For international strategists watching from Washington and Brussels, tonight’s results will reveal whether the populist right’s advance into local government is a durable structural shift or a protest peak — a question with direct parallels to MAGA’s grip on the American GOP.

Sources: Euronews · YouGov · PollCheck · New Statesman

Global Security

Israel Bombs Beirut, Shattering a Three-Week Ceasefire

Israel struck Beirut’s southern suburbs on Wednesday for the first time since the April 17 ceasefire, targeting the head of Hezbollah’s elite Radwan Force in a direct hit on the Dahieh neighbourhood. At least 13 people were killed across Lebanon on the same day, including a village mayor, his wife, and two children in a separate strike on Zellaya in the Bekaa Valley. The Israeli military also expanded displacement orders deeper into the Bekaa, while drone activity intensified over central Beirut. The timing is politically explosive: the strike came hours after Trump announced progress toward an Iran deal, suggesting that Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu may be seeking to demonstrate that Israeli operations will continue regardless of any US-Iran arrangement. For the broader campaign landscape, the Beirut strike complicates Trump’s peace narrative — his closest ally in the region is actively escalating while the White House talks of de-escalation. Democrats will frame the dissonance as proof that Trump cannot control the war he started; Republicans will argue Israel is acting independently in self-defence.

Sources: Al Jazeera · The National · CBC

Michigan’s Greene Wins by 20 Points, Democrats Keep State Senate

Democrat Chedrick Greene, a Saginaw fire captain and former US Marine, won Michigan’s closely watched State Senate District 35 special election Tuesday night with 60 percent of the vote, defeating Republican Jason Tunney and preserving Democrats’ 20–18 majority in the chamber. The district had been without representation for 16 months after Democrat Kristen McDonald Rivet was elected to Congress, and both parties treated it as a bellwether: Kamala Harris won it by just 1,000 votes in 2024, making it precisely the kind of blue-collar, swing-district terrain where Trump has been gaining. Greene’s 20-point margin in that environment exceeded every Democratic projection and immediately reframed the midterm conversation. With Senate control secured through year’s end, Democrats can now pass the Fiscal Year 2027 budget without Republican votes. National operatives took note: PBS reported that the result may offer clues about what is coming in November. For Republican strategists, the question is whether the Michigan result reflects a genuine enthusiasm gap or a special-election turnout artefact that will not replicate at scale.

Sources: Michigan Advance · NBC News · PBS

AI in Politics · Ongoing: AI Regulation

Connecticut Passes America’s Most Comprehensive AI Law

Connecticut’s legislature has sent Governor Ned Lamont the most sweeping artificial-intelligence statute in the country — and this time, unlike last year’s veto-killed effort, the governor says he will sign it. Senate Bill 5, the Artificial Intelligence Responsibility and Transparency Act, passed the House 131–17 on May 1 after clearing the Senate 32–4, with robust bipartisan support. The law covers an unusually broad surface area: frontier AI model developers face transparency obligations; chatbot operators must adopt protocols to detect self-harm and disclose when users are communicating with an AI; social media companies are barred from exposing minors to addictive algorithms without parental consent; and employers deploying AI in hiring, promotion, or discipline must meet disclosure and compliance requirements starting October 1, 2026. The chatbot provisions are especially relevant for political technology: any AI system that engages voters in conversation — the model pioneered by tools like Convos in Wisconsin — would need to disclose its non-human nature and implement safety protocols. Connecticut is thus legislating the very gap that yesterday’s First Coffee spread on AI voter persuasion identified as unregulated. The bill’s passage also reignites the federal pre-emption debate: with Connecticut joining Colorado, the patchwork of state AI laws is now thick enough to create serious compliance costs for national campaigns, strengthening the industry argument for a single federal standard.

Sources: CT Mirror · Freshfields · GovTech

AI in Politics · Ongoing: AI Industry Political Spending

AI Super PACs Commit $225 Million to Shape Who Regulates Them

The artificial-intelligence industry’s political war chest for the 2026 midterms has crossed a quarter of a billion dollars and is growing. Leading the Future, the pro-AI super PAC backed by OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman and Andreessen Horowitz, has committed more than $100 million to federal races supporting candidates who favour light-touch regulation. Separately, Anthropic announced a $20 million donation to Public First Action, backing candidates who favour AI guardrails — a pointed split in the industry between the build-fast and build-safe camps. A third grouping, backed by Trump allies, has launched its own $100 million pro-AI spending push aimed at electing candidates who will block state-level regulation entirely. NBC News reports that the ads flooding swing-state airwaves are about everything except AI itself — attacking opponents on immigration, crime, and inflation while quietly advancing the industry’s regulatory preferences. For campaign finance watchers, the AI industry’s midterm spend now rivals the fossil-fuel sector’s peak political investment, with one critical difference: the regulated industry is trying to choose its regulators before the rules are written.

Sources: ABC News · NBC News · Fox News · Quartz

Global Elections

Scotland and Wales Go to the Polls: SNP Eyes Fifth Victory, Labour Braces for Plaid

Beyond the English council elections, today’s UK vote includes two contests with profound constitutional implications. In Scotland, the SNP is forecast to win a fifth consecutive Holyrood victory and may secure an outright majority — a result that would reignite the independence debate just as Westminster’s authority is being tested by Reform’s English advance. In Wales, voters are electing a dramatically reshaped Senedd: the chamber has expanded from 60 to 96 seats under a new proportional party-list system, the most significant electoral reform in Welsh history. Labour, which has governed Wales continuously since 1999, is projected to lose power for the first time, with Plaid Cymru and Reform UK both leading in recent polls. The Senedd reform itself is a live political issue: critics call it an establishment power grab that voters never asked for; supporters argue proportional representation is long overdue. For international observers, the triple election — English councils, Scottish Parliament, Welsh Senedd — offers a single-day snapshot of how four distinct electorates are responding to the same economic headwinds, immigration pressures, and anti-establishment sentiment, each through a different electoral system.

AI in Politics · Ongoing: AI Regulation

Colorado Scraps Its Landmark AI Act and Starts Over

Colorado, the state that passed America’s first comprehensive AI law in 2024, is now dismantling it before it takes effect. Senate Majority Leader Robert Rodriguez has introduced legislation to repeal and replace the Colorado AI Act with a fundamentally different framework — one that swaps algorithmic-discrimination reporting, mandatory risk assessments, and impact evaluations for a lighter regime centred on transparency, recordkeeping, and consumer rights. Governor Jared Polis, who signed the original law reluctantly and publicly warned it could chase businesses out of the state, has backed the rewrite through his AI Policy Work Group. The replacement, the Automated Decision Making Technology Framework, would take effect January 1, 2027, giving companies a full year to adjust. The original act was scheduled to become enforceable on June 30, 2026 — barely eight weeks from now. The Colorado reversal sends a signal that reverberates beyond state borders: if the most ambitious AI regulation in America cannot survive contact with political and industry reality, what does that mean for Connecticut’s just-passed law, or for the dozens of state AI bills still in committee? For campaign compliance teams, the instability creates planning paralysis — the rules that apply to AI-driven voter outreach may change again before November.

Sources: Axios Denver · Colorado Politics · Mayer Brown

European AI & Cloud Summit Cologne: 3,000 Gather as EU AI Act Deadlines Bite

Europe’s largest conference dedicated to artificial intelligence and cloud computing wraps today in Cologne, where over 3,000 IT professionals, developers, and policymakers have spent three days grappling with the practical realities of EU AI Act compliance. The summit, running May 5–7, is the latest in a spring conference circuit that has included Rise of AI Berlin’s tenth-anniversary edition earlier this week and will continue with Deep Tech Momentum Berlin on May 20–21. What unites them is urgency: the first binding obligations of the EU AI Act took effect in February 2026, and companies deploying high-risk AI systems face full compliance deadlines by August. Political technology vendors are a specific focus — AI systems used in voter targeting, political advertising, and election administration fall squarely within the Act’s high-risk classification. For American campaign technology firms eyeing European markets, or European parties adopting US-built AI tools, the compliance clock is ticking. Unlike the fragmented US approach — where Connecticut just passed a law and Colorado is scrapping one — the EU AI Act offers a single, continent-wide standard. Whether that standard becomes a model or a cautionary tale depends on what happens in these rooms over the next ninety days.

Sources: Community Days · Rise of AI · Deep Tech Week

Democrats See a Midterm Playbook Forming — From Michigan to the Map

Chedrick Greene’s 60-percent performance in a district Kamala Harris won by a single point is the latest data point in a pattern Democratic strategists are assembling into a midterm narrative. The Michigan special election follows Wisconsin’s April Supreme Court race, where Democrat Susan Crawford won statewide, and a string of overperformances in special elections since January. PBS and NBC both noted that the Michigan result may preview what November looks like in similar blue-collar swing districts. The emerging Democratic playbook has three pillars: recruit candidates with local credibility and non-political résumés (Greene is a fire captain and Marine veteran); run against Washington dysfunction rather than defending national Democratic leadership (Iowa’s Zach Wahls has made anti-Schumer sentiment the centrepiece of his Senate bid); and exploit the enthusiasm gap that special elections are revealing. The risk for Democrats is over-reading low-turnout results: special elections attract motivated partisans, and midterms with Trump on the ballot as a motivator for both bases will produce a very different electorate. For Republican strategists, the counter-argument is simple — Indiana proved that Trump’s endorsement machine still works when it is aimed at the right targets.

Sources: PBS · NBC News · CNN

Is Reform UK’s Rise Democratic Renewal or Populist Fragmentation?

“Reform Is Giving a Voice to Voters the Mainstream Abandoned”

Supporters of Reform UK’s local election surge argue it represents a long-overdue democratic correction. For decades, millions of voters in post-industrial towns and rural England felt unrepresented by a London-centric Labour Party and a managerial Conservative Party. Reform’s projected gains — from three council seats to over 1,300 — reflect genuine demand, not manufactured populism. If established parties had addressed concerns about immigration, housing, and cultural identity, Reform would not exist. Local government is exactly where new movements should prove themselves: accountable, visible, and forced to deliver. Denying Reform’s democratic legitimacy because its politics are uncomfortable is itself anti-democratic.

Sources: RTÉ · UK in a Changing Europe

“A Protest Vote Is Not a Governing Mandate”

Critics counter that Reform’s surge is a product of protest, not policy readiness. The party has near-zero experience running councils, no established candidate pipeline, and a manifesto light on local governance detail. Historic parallels are cautionary: UKIP won 3.9 million votes in 2015 but collapsed when asked to govern. A three-way split between Reform, Labour, and the Conservatives risks paralysing local government at precisely the moment councils face severe funding crises. Moreover, Reform’s gains in places like Sunderland and Barnsley come at Labour’s expense, potentially fragmenting the progressive vote in a way that benefits the Conservative Party nationally. Democratic renewal requires more than channelling anger — it requires institutional capacity that Reform has yet to demonstrate.

Sources: Economics Observatory · British Brief

Political Tech Application

Grassroots Analytics + Quiller AI — The All-in-One AI Fundraising Machine

Grassroots Analytics, the progressive data firm known for its donor segmentation infrastructure, has acquired Quiller AI, the generative-AI fundraising platform named Best New AI Tool by Campaigns & Elections. The merged entity combines Quiller’s ability to generate first-draft fundraising emails in under 20 seconds with Grassroots Analytics’ granular donor-behaviour data, creating what the companies call an all-in-one AI fundraising tool that drives meaningful action.

Quiller was founded by Mike Nellis, CEO of Authentic Campaigns, and incubated at Higher Ground Labs. Before the acquisition, it had been used by Democratic campaigns and committees to draft and deploy email, SMS, and narrative content — producing copy that its developers described as 70 percent of the way to a finished draft. The integration with Grassroots Analytics means the AI can now personalise not just the language of a fundraising appeal but the targeting, timing, and ask amount based on individual donor history. No equivalent integrated platform currently exists on the Republican side, though GOP-aligned firms are building competing tools.

> takeaway: The Grassroots-Quiller merger creates the first closed-loop AI fundraising system in politics — data informs the AI, the AI generates the ask, the response feeds new data back into the model. If the integration delivers on its promise, it could widen the fundraising gap between parties that adopt AI early and those that do not.

Sources: Grassroots Analytics · Quiller.ai · Campaigns & Elections
