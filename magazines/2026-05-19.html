01 · Research Insights of the Day

Five AI Chatbots, One Recommendation: The Communist Party

Andrew Hall et al. · Stanford Freeman Spogli Institute · April 2026

During Japan's snap Lower House election on February 8, 2026, Stanford researchers queried five major AI models from OpenAI, Google, and xAI, asking each to recommend a political party based on synthetic voter profiles. The results were striking: every model converged on recommending the Japanese Communist Party to voters with left-leaning policy positions, even though multiple other parties hold broadly similar stances on the issues tested. The bias appears driven by which information sources the models can access and how they weight policy-position matching, rather than any deliberate ideological tilt. The finding extends earlier work on AI persuasion into a new domain: not just swaying opinions, but funneling undecided voters toward specific parties through opaque recommendation logic.

Practitioner implication: Any campaign deploying AI-based voter-advice tools must audit recommendation outputs across the full political spectrum before launch. Unexamined model defaults can concentrate referrals on fringe parties, distorting competitive dynamics in ways no human advisor would.

Source: Stanford FSI / APARC, April 2026 · aparc.fsi.stanford.edu

Trump Pivots to Midterms After Beijing Summit Yields Few Deals

Days after returning from a high-profile state visit to Beijing — his first trip to China since 2017 — President Trump and senior officials are fanning out across the country this week for events touting domestic achievements. The pivot is unmistakable: after months dominated by the Iran war, the China summit, and diplomatic crises in Cuba and Venezuela, the White House is racing to reframe the midterm narrative around affordability and economic wins.

The Beijing meetings ended with spectacle but few concrete outcomes. Meanwhile, a CNBC report quotes a Democratic strategist summarizing the political terrain bluntly: voters are angry about the economy. Trump was elected on a promise to tame inflation and reduce costs, and with gas above four dollars a gallon and grocery prices elevated, that promise remains unfulfilled in the eyes of the electorate. The administration now faces a six-month sprint to persuade swing-district voters that the economic trajectory is improving — all while managing an unpopular foreign conflict with no clear exit ramp.

04 · Political Campaigning

67 Million Records: America’s Largest Voter-Roll Purge

The Trump administration has run at least 67 million registered voter names through an expanded version of the SAVE (Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements) system, originally designed to check immigration status for benefit applications. Of those records, roughly 24,000 were flagged as potential non-citizens and 350,000 as deceased — but critics warn that even a small error rate at that scale means thousands of legitimate voters could be removed before November.

The Washington Post has documented cases of naturalized citizens flagged because their immigration records were not updated. With voter-registration deadlines falling in September and October, the window for a wrongly purged voter to discover the problem and navigate the appeals process is dangerously narrow. Voting-rights groups from the Campaign Legal Center to Protect Democracy have filed legal challenges, arguing the program violates the National Voter Registration Act’s 90-day quiet period. Republicans and the administration counter that the purges target only genuinely ineligible registrations and are essential to election integrity.

Washington Post, May 17 · PBS NewsHour, May 17 · Campaign Legal Center

05 · GovTech

Florida Redraws the Map: Four New GOP Seats After VRA Ruling

Florida has become the eighth state to complete mid-decade redistricting in the 2026 cycle, and the most consequential. Governor DeSantis proposed a new congressional map during a special legislative session, and lawmakers passed it within days — just hours after the Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act in a Louisiana case. The new lines target four incumbent Democrats: Kathy Castor in Tampa, Darren Soto in Orlando, Lois Frankel in West Palm Beach, and Debbie Wasserman Schultz in Fort Lauderdale.

Ongoing: The VRA fallout continues to ripple across the national map. Voting-rights groups have already filed suit, arguing the Florida plan violates the state constitution’s anti-gerrymandering provision. But with the federal guardrail weakened, the legal path for challengers is narrower than ever. Florida’s four new Republican-leaning seats could prove decisive in determining which party controls the House after November.

NBC News · Democracy Docket · Votebeat

$4.48 a Gallon: How the Iran War Reshapes the Midterm Battlefield

Sources: NPR, Bloomberg, Axios, CNBC · May 2026

Before the first American missile struck Iranian air defenses on February 28, the average price of a gallon of regular gasoline in the United States sat comfortably below three dollars. Twelve weeks later it has climbed to four dollars and forty-eight cents nationally, a spike of roughly fifty percent that has made the conflict’s cost visceral at every fuel pump in the country. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed. Crude oil trades above one hundred dollars a barrel. And the political consequences are landing squarely on the party in power.

An NPR/PBS poll released May 6 found that sixty-three percent of respondents blame President Trump for the increase in gas prices, including a third of self-identified Republicans. Only twenty-nine percent of Americans approve of the strikes on Iran, and two-thirds — including forty-four percent of Republicans — expect prices to keep climbing. The generic congressional ballot in the same survey showed Democrats leading fifty-two to forty-two, a margin that, if it held through November, would signal a wave election.

The Iran war’s fuel-price shock is hitting hardest in the very states that will decide Senate control. Axios reported in March that the Senate battleground map overlaps almost perfectly with the states where gas-price sensitivity is highest. Affordability was already the Democrats’ central midterm message; the cost of an unpopular war now visible at the pump has supercharged it.

The White House response has been reactive. Trump told CNBC in April that gas prices could end up roughly where they are or perhaps a bit higher by the midterms. Congressional Republicans are now floating a federal gas-tax holiday — an idea many of them once derided as a fiscal gimmick — as the most visible short-term relief available. Whether an eighteen-cent-per-gallon savings can counter the broader narrative of a war-driven price shock remains the open question of the midterm cycle.

Ongoing: Colombia’s Runoff Math Tilts Against the Ruling Party

With twelve days until the May 31 first round, the race to become Colombia’s next president has entered its most volatile phase. Ruling-party candidate Ivan Cepeda of Pacto Historico leads comfortably with between 37 and 44 percent across major polls, virtually assuring him a place in the June 21 runoff. But the identity of his opponent now matters more than his own lead: a Genesis Crea Foundation poll puts Centro Democratico senator Paloma Valencia at 25.4 percent, narrowing the gap with attorney Abelardo de la Espriella at 21.6 percent.

The significance is in the runoff projections. Weighted averages of recent polling suggest Cepeda could narrowly defeat De la Espriella head-to-head — but would lose to Valencia. The right’s best hope, in other words, is consolidation behind Valencia before the first round even begins. The next two weeks of campaigning will determine whether that consolidation happens or whether the anti-Petro vote remains fatally split.

08 · AI in Politics

Colorado Guts Its Own Landmark AI Law After Two Years of Struggle

The fiercest state-level fight over AI regulation in the country ended with a whimper. On May 12, the Colorado legislature passed Senate Bill 189 by overwhelming bipartisan margins — 34-1 in the Senate, 57-6 in the House — repealing and replacing the state’s first-in-the-nation AI Act that was signed just two years ago. Governor Jared Polis, who had vetoed an earlier iteration and spent months brokering the compromise, signed the bill within hours.

The new law dramatically scales back requirements. Instead of mandating that companies disclose how their AI systems contribute to high-stakes decisions on hiring, lending, and housing, it simply requires notification to consumers that AI is being used. The implementation deadline has been pushed from June 2026 to January 2027. For advocates who saw Colorado as the vanguard of meaningful AI governance, SB 189 reads as a capitulation. For the tech industry, it reads as a pragmatic compromise that avoids a patchwork of unworkable state mandates.

Colorado Sun, May 12 · CPR News

09 · GovTech

CISA CI Fortify: Critical Infrastructure Told to Prepare for Weeks of Isolation

On May 5, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency released CI Fortify, a guidance framework that tells operators of critical infrastructure to plan for operating without internet, third-party vendors, or telecommunications for weeks to months during a conflict scenario. The guidance is blunt: operators should assume that threat actors will have some access to their operational-technology networks and that all external connections will be unreliable.

CI Fortify urges two core capabilities: proactive isolation (disconnecting from business networks to sustain essential services in a degraded environment) and recovery (documenting systems, backing up critical files, and practicing manual fallback). The initiative signals a shift in how the federal government frames infrastructure security — moving from breach prevention to operational continuity under sustained attack. With the Iran conflict already raising fears of retaliatory cyber operations, the timing is pointed: this is not a theoretical exercise.

CISA, May 5 · CyberScoop

Higher Ground Labs Fund V: Betting on Agentic AI as Core Campaign Infrastructure

The progressive political-technology accelerator Higher Ground Labs has launched Fund V with a new thesis: AI is no longer a campaign add-on but foundational infrastructure. The rolling fund — open to year-round submissions — is targeting three investment areas: strategic new bets that evolve the campaign playbook, citizen-centered relationship systems that sustain voter engagement over time, and community-led messaging platforms that surface authentic grassroots insights.

The fund is led by Hillary Lehr, returning after overseeing the successful acquisition of HGL-incubated startup Quiller AI by Grassroots Analytics. The emphasis on agentic AI — systems that can adapt in real time, run feedback loops, and autonomously execute campaign tasks — marks a departure from earlier bets on static analytics and simple automation. Whether progressive campaigns can field these tools at scale before November remains the central question.

TikTok Leads All Platforms in Political Misinformation, EU Study Finds

A new European Union study measuring misinformation exposure across major social platforms found TikTok at the top of the table: roughly one in five exposure-weighted posts on the platform contains misinformation spanning health, climate, migration, and national politics. Facebook followed at 13 percent, X/Twitter at 11, YouTube and Instagram at around 8 percent each, and LinkedIn at just 2 percent.

The findings land as TikTok navigates its new ownership structure — a joint venture giving Oracle, Silver Lake, and other U.S. investors a 45 percent stake in a new American entity. The platform has expanded its use of automated moderation, claiming that over 85 percent of content removed for guideline violations is caught by AI before any human sees it. But the EU data suggests the problem is not one of takedown speed; it is one of ambient exposure, where misinformation spreads virally before moderation systems can intervene.

12 · GovTech

DeSantis Signs Data-Center Bill: Shielding Consumers from the AI Boom’s Power Costs

Governor Ron DeSantis has signed legislation regulating large-scale data centers in Florida, directly addressing one of the less-discussed costs of the AI revolution: energy. The bill promises that Florida consumers will not bear the burden of the AI boom through higher electric bills or scarcer water resources. Data centers have become voracious consumers of both, and as hyperscale facilities proliferate to meet AI compute demand, states are beginning to draw regulatory lines.

Florida’s move positions the state alongside Virginia and several others now grappling with the infrastructure demands of generative AI. The bill establishes energy-use disclosure requirements and environmental-impact thresholds for new data-center construction. For the broader GovTech landscape, it signals a new category of AI governance — not regulating what models do, but regulating the physical footprint of the systems that run them.

Two Perspectives on One Issue

The Federal Gas Tax Holiday: Gimmick or Emergency Relief?

The Case Against: A Bipartisan Gimmick

Critics across the political spectrum argue that suspending the 18.4-cent federal gas tax is a band-aid on a bullet wound. The Bipartisan Policy Center estimates the actual consumer savings would be 10 to 16 cents per gallon — a fraction of the war-driven price increase. A five-month pause would drain roughly $17 billion from the Highway Trust Fund, roughly 46 percent of its annual revenue, accelerating its projected insolvency and threatening road and bridge projects nationwide. The Washington Post editorial board called it a gimmick that addresses symptoms while ignoring the structural cause: an unpopular military conflict with no ceasefire in sight.

Washington Post Opinion, May 11 · Bipartisan Policy Center · Time, May 12

The Case For: Emergency Relief Now

Proponents frame the gas-tax holiday as the fastest available lever in a crisis. Senator Josh Hawley’s Gas Tax Suspension Act would pause both the 18.4-cent gas tax and the 24.4-cent diesel tax for 90 to 180 days. Bipartisan support has emerged: Democratic Senators Mark Kelly and Richard Blumenthal have sponsored a parallel bill suspending the tax through October 1. Supporters argue that in a moment when two-thirds of Americans expect gas prices to keep rising and voters are punishing incumbents at the ballot box, even modest relief signals that Washington is not indifferent to the pain at the pump.

CNBC, May 11 · Fox Business · U.S. News, May 12

14 · Political Tech Application

$ quiller-ai — grassroots-analytics

What it does: Quiller AI is a generative-AI copilot built for political campaigns and nonprofits. It drafts fundraising emails, volunteer scripts, op-eds, and mobilization copy — producing first drafts that campaign staff describe as seventy percent complete, ready for human polish with candidate-specific details, local references, and tonal adjustments.

Who is using it: Originally incubated by Higher Ground Labs and founded by Democratic strategist Mike Nellis, Quiller won the 2024 Reed Award for Best New AI Tool. In August 2025, Grassroots Analytics acquired Quiller, integrating its generative capabilities into a broader suite of donor analytics, targeting, and outreach tools. The combined platform now serves Democratic campaigns and progressive advocacy organizations at scale during the 2026 midterm cycle.

Measured outcome: Campaigns using Quiller report cutting email-production time by more than half, allowing small teams to maintain high-frequency donor communication that previously required dedicated copywriting staff. The integration with Grassroots Analytics adds predictive donor scoring, meaning the AI drafts are targeted to the audiences most likely to convert.

takeaway: The Quiller acquisition exemplifies the consolidation phase in progressive political technology — point solutions absorbed into platforms, AI copilots becoming table stakes, and the campaign-software market maturing from tool bazaar to integrated suite.
