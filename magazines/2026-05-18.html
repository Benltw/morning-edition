Chatbots Are Biased and Should Not Be Used for Political Advice

Stephanie Brandl, Mathias Wessel Tromborg & Frederik Hjorth · University of Copenhagen / Aarhus University · April 2026

Popular AI chatbots including ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama, Mistral, and Qwen systematically cluster at the centre or centre-left of the political spectrum when tested against real-world election candidate questionnaires, according to a new analysis from the National Centre for AI in Society (CAISA) at the University of Copenhagen. Using Altinget’s official candidate test from the 2022 Danish general election, the researchers found that every model they tested recommended parties in the Social Democratic or Alternative camp more frequently than right-of-centre parties — a pattern that held regardless of prompt framing. Biases manifest in subtle but consequential ways: which arguments are emphasised, which caveats are omitted, and which parties are surfaced first. Critically, most chatbots are trained predominantly on English-language, American-centric data, meaning their apparent knowledge of smaller democracies may be shallow even as their recommendations appear authoritative.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns and civic organisations should treat chatbot-generated voter guidance as structurally biased. Any tool that steers citizens toward specific parties — even unintentionally — raises serious questions about whether AI belongs in the democratic advice space at all.

University of Copenhagen / CAISA, published April 2026; also covered by Phys.org and Digital Information World.

SCOTUS Guts the Voting Rights Act; Thousands Rally in the Birthplace of Civil Rights

The Supreme Court has fundamentally rewritten the rules for challenging racial gerrymandering. In Louisiana v. Callais, the Court ruled that plaintiffs must now prove intentional discrimination — not merely discriminatory effect — to challenge legislative maps under Section 2 of the 1965 Voting Rights Act. Within days, the Court used the new standard to reverse a 2023 injunction against Alabama’s congressional map, a map it had previously deemed racially discriminatory. Governor Kay Ivey immediately set special primary elections under the restored map. The response was swift: on Saturday more than 5,000 people gathered at the Alabama Capitol in Montgomery for the “All Roads Lead to the South” rally, with parallel marches in Selma. Bernice King, CEO of the King Center, spoke alongside Senator Booker and local organisers. The mobilisation marks the largest voting-rights demonstration since the 2020 John Lewis marches and signals that redistricting will be a defining issue of the 2026 midterms. The ruling effectively raises the evidentiary bar for every pending redistricting challenge in the country.

Sources: PBS News, 16 May 2026; Alabama Reflector, 12 & 16 May 2026; Democracy Docket; Washington Post, 11 May 2026; Spectrum News

Philippines Opens Duterte Impeachment Trial — the First Double Impeachment in History

The Philippine Senate convenes today as an impeachment court for the trial of Vice President Sara Duterte, making her the first official in the country’s history to face impeachment proceedings twice. The House voted 257–26 on May 11 to send articles alleging misuse of confidential funds, unexplained wealth, and threats against President Marcos and former House Speaker Romualdez. A first impeachment in 2025 secured 215 votes but was later voided by the Supreme Court on procedural grounds. The trial’s prospects remain uncertain: newly elected Senate President Alan Peter Cayetano — who served as foreign secretary under Duterte’s father, Rodrigo — controls the calendar and the rules. Removal requires a two-thirds Senate majority, a threshold that looks difficult given the chamber’s recent leadership shift toward Duterte-aligned senators. The case is being watched across Southeast Asia as a test of whether institutional accountability can survive dynastic politics.

Sources: Al Jazeera, 11 May 2026; Rappler, May 2026; PBS NewsHour; The Diplomat; Philippine News Agency; Inquirer

AI Super PACs Pour $100M+ into Midterms — on Everything Except AI

Leading the Future, the pro-AI super PAC bankrolled by Andreessen Horowitz and OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman, has banked over $125 million and begun spending aggressively in Texas and North Carolina primaries. Its ads attack or support candidates based on immigration, crime, and cultural issues — with no mention whatsoever of artificial intelligence. The strategy is deliberate: the PAC’s goal is to elect lawmakers sympathetic to light-touch AI regulation, but its messaging avoids the topic entirely, betting that voters care more about kitchen-table issues than tech policy. On the other side, a bipartisan pair of pro-regulation super PACs led by former Representatives Chris Stewart (R-UT) and Brad Carson (D-OK), with $20 million from Anthropic, is backing candidates who favour stricter oversight. The AI industry’s total midterm spend is projected to exceed what the crypto sector invested in 2024 — and the fight is still in its opening rounds. NBC News reports at least 15 AI-generated campaign ads have already aired across multiple states.

Sources: NBC News, Feb 2026; NOTUS; Built In; Popular.info; Axios, 8 May 2026; ABC News; CNBC

Harvard Youth Poll: Gen Z Trust in Government Falls to Record Low

The 52nd edition of the Harvard Institute of Politics Youth Poll, released in late April, finds young Americans under intense economic pressure and increasingly losing faith in democratic institutions. Trust in the federal government has fallen to 15 percent — the lowest level since the poll began in 2000. Housing costs and inflation dominate concerns, and widespread scepticism about electoral fairness suggests midterm turnout may be shaped more by doubt than by partisan preference. Democrats still hold a wide lead on the generic congressional ballot among registered young voters (45% to 26%), with double-digit advantages across nearly every major subgroup. But approval ratings for Congressional Democrats (26%) and Republicans (25%) are virtually identical — a historically unusual convergence that suggests young voters are choosing the lesser of two distrusted options. The largest share of respondents (37%) said their top priority in a candidate is shared values; 17% want independence from special interest groups.

Sources: Harvard IOP, 52nd Edition, Spring 2026; Boston Globe, 24 Apr 2026; Harvard Crimson; Cook Political Report; Fortune

Progressives Bet AI Anxiety Will Win Them Elections

A growing cohort of progressive candidates is turning artificial intelligence into a bread-and-butter campaign issue, framing AI regulation as worker protection, not tech policy. Michigan state Senator Mallory McMorrow is campaigning on AI oversight, arguing the issue can attract young, rural, and working-class voters whom Democrats have struggled to reach. A March NBC News poll found 57% of registered voters believe AI’s risks outweigh its benefits. The politics are scrambling traditional coalitions: socialist Senator Bernie Sanders and conservative Governor Ron DeSantis have both emerged as leading opponents of AI data-centre construction, drawing populist energy from opposite ends of the spectrum. But the path is fraught. Representative Pramila Jayapal warns that AI industry spending is silencing Democratic voices who favour regulation. With Leading the Future and allied PACs pouring tens of millions into primaries, progressives who take on the industry risk being massively outspent — even as the public opinion they’re courting appears squarely on their side.

Sources: NOTUS, May 2026; Time; Schneier on Security; Fortune/NBC News Poll, Mar 2026; Built In

Ongoing: Colombia — Valencia Surges to Second as Runoff Math Tightens

With thirteen days until Colombia’s May 31 first round, the presidential race has crystallised into a three-way contest — but the real drama is in the runoff arithmetic. Left-wing senator Iván Cepeda, the political heir of President Gustavo Petro, holds a commanding first-round lead: the latest Invamer survey puts him at 44.3%, with attorney Abelardo de la Espriella at 21.5% and centre-right senator Paloma Valencia at 19.8%. Valencia has nearly doubled her support since the last poll and is now the fastest-rising candidate in the field, with particularly strong gains among urban professionals. The critical question is who finishes second. Against de la Espriella in a runoff, Cepeda wins comfortably. Against Valencia, the margin narrows to a statistical toss-up: one Fundación Génesis Crea survey shows 51.2% to 46.6%. Markets are watching closely — a Valencia runoff could unsettle investors anticipating Cepeda continuity with the Petro programme. AtlasIntel and Guarumo continue to diverge on the second-place fight, ensuring the final fortnight will be volatile.

Sources: AS/COA Poll Tracker; Rio Times Online; City Paper Bogotá; ColombiaOne, 16 May 2026; Wikipedia; Fundación Génesis Crea

Nebraska’s Blue Dot: Powell Wins, Protects the Electoral Vote

Omaha, Nebraska · 13 May 2026

Political organiser Denise Powell has won the Democratic primary for Nebraska’s 2nd Congressional District, the “blue dot” that allocates one of the state’s five electoral votes independently — and which handed that vote to Joe Biden in 2020 and Kamala Harris in 2024. Powell defeated state Senator John Cavanaugh by a 39%–37% margin in a seven-candidate field, in a race that turned from cordial to contentious in its final weeks as both front-runners launched negative ads. The outcome carries consequences well beyond the district. Cavanaugh’s departure from the state Senate would have created a vacancy fillable by Nebraska’s Republican governor — potentially enabling the legislature to switch the state to winner-take-all electoral allocation, eliminating the blue dot entirely. Powell, backed by EMILY’s List, the Congressional Black Caucus, and the Congressional Hispanic Caucus BOLD PAC, was seen as the more moderate choice. The progressive Cavanaugh held endorsements from the Congressional Progressive Caucus PAC and nearly a dozen local unions. Powell now faces Republican Omaha City Council member Brinker Harding in November in what Cook Political Report rates a Toss Up.

Sources: NPR, 13 May 2026; NBC News; CNN; Nebraska Examiner; Nebraska Public Media; Omaha World-Herald

Nature Study: AI Chatbots Sway Voters Four Times Better Than TV Ads

Brief AI chatbot conversations can shift voter attitudes by up to 10 percentage points — roughly four times the measured effect of traditional political advertisements from the 2016 and 2020 U.S. elections. That is the headline finding from twin papers published in Nature and Science by a Cornell-led team that ran pre-registered experiments during the 2024 U.S. presidential election and the 2025 Canadian and Polish elections. The mechanism is surprisingly mundane: the models persuade by marshalling relevant facts, not by deploying sophisticated psychological manipulation. When researchers blocked the models from citing factual claims, persuasion effects collapsed. A troubling asymmetry emerged: chatbots instructed to advocate for right-leaning candidates produced more factual inaccuracies than those advocating for left-leaning candidates — a pattern that held across all three countries. The findings arrive as AI-powered voter outreach tools are being deployed at scale in the 2026 midterms, adding empirical weight to concerns that conversational AI could reshape electoral dynamics in ways traditional media regulation was never designed to address.

Sources: Nature, Dec 2025; Science, Dec 2025; MIT Technology Review; Scientific American; Cornell Chronicle; AEI

Grok Breaks Ranks: The Only Chatbot Willing to Endorse Candidates

While ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude carefully dodge direct electoral recommendations, Elon Musk’s Grok has staked out a singular position in the AI landscape: it will tell you who to vote for. The xAI chatbot endorsed Republican candidate Steve Hilton in the California governor’s primary, a recommendation that no other mainstream model would touch. An SF Standard investigation found that Grok is alone among major chatbots in making explicit candidate endorsements — a feature, not a bug, of its positioning as the anti-censorship alternative. But Grok’s political identity is unstable: on the Political Compass Test it registers a clear rightward shift, yet on Pew’s political typology quiz it lands as an “establishment liberal.” A Global Witness investigation found Grok responds to political queries with disinformation and toxic content. The University of Copenhagen research (see Spread 01) confirms the broader pattern: all tested chatbots carry political bias, but Grok is the only one that wears it openly. The question for regulators is whether transparent bias is more or less dangerous than hidden bias.

Sources: SF Standard, 13 May 2026; Global Witness; University of Copenhagen/CAISA, Apr 2026; Built In

Who Regulates AI? Florida’s DeSantis vs. Silicon Valley’s Super PACs

The Case for State-Level Regulation

Governor Ron DeSantis pushed Florida’s “AI Bill of Rights” to ban companion chatbots for minors, require bots to disclose their non-human status, and restrict AI tools in elementary schools. He frames regulation as protecting families from corporate overreach, positioning the issue as populist consumer protection. After the legislature killed the bill, DeSantis accused House Republicans of serving a “Big Tech cartel.” The emerging left-right populist alliance on AI regulation — with Sanders and DeSantis on the same side — represents a rare ideological convergence.

Sources: NBC News; Florida Phoenix, 28 Apr 2026; Fox Business; WLRN; Route Fifty

The Case for Federal Preemption

Florida House Speaker Daniel Perez refused to file DeSantis’s AI bills, arguing that regulation should be left to the federal government — a position aligned with the Trump administration’s executive order and pending federal legislation that would limit states’ ability to restrict AI. The White House directly contacted Perez to discourage the Florida AI Bill of Rights. Leading the Future and allied super PACs are spending tens of millions to elect lawmakers who support industry self-governance and a unified national framework, arguing that a patchwork of state laws would cripple American AI competitiveness.

Sources: Florida Phoenix, 28 Apr 2026; NBC News; NOTUS; Built In; Popular.info

$ aaru --synthetic-voters

Aaru is an AI-powered synthetic polling platform that replaces traditional survey panels with artificial voter agents. Using Census Bureau demographic data, the system replicates entire voter districts as populations of AI agents, each programmed to mirror the attitudes, values, and likely behaviour of the voters they represent. A typical survey draws on roughly 5,000 synthetic respondents and completes in 30 to 90 seconds — at approximately one-tenth the cost of a traditional phone or online poll. The platform gained credibility after predicting the 2024 New York State Democratic primary within 371 votes of the actual result. It is now being used by Republican campaigns and consulting firms to rapidly test messaging, simulate voter reactions to breaking news, and identify emerging public opinion trends. The National Republican Senatorial Committee has integrated AI tools including Aaru-style synthetic polling into its data and ad operations for the 2026 cycle, part of a broader GOP bet on AI-augmented campaigning.

> takeaway: Synthetic polling collapses the feedback loop between event and response from weeks to seconds. If the accuracy holds at scale, it may render traditional midterm polling obsolete — and give early adopters a significant tactical advantage in rapidly shifting media environments.

Sources: Axios, 14 Apr 2026; Semafor, Sep 2024; LinkedIn/Leonard Murphy; AI CERTs News; RealClearMarkets, Apr 2026
