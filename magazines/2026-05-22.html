The Levers of Political Persuasion with Conversational Artificial Intelligence

Bai et al. -- Science, 2026

A large-scale experimental study published in Science examined approximately 77,000 voters in Britain across 700 political issues to measure how effectively AI chatbots can shift political opinions compared with traditional campaign tools. The central finding is stark: a single chatbot conversation changed voters' positions at roughly four times the rate of watching televised campaign advertisements, the previous gold standard of political persuasion research.

Critically, the researchers identified which technical levers drive the effect. Post-training alignment methods boosted persuasiveness by as much as 51 percent, while prompt engineering added another 27 percent. By contrast, scaling model size and personalizing messages to individual voter profiles contributed far less than expected. The study also surfaced a troubling trade-off: the methods that made chatbots more persuasive systematically decreased their factual accuracy, with right-leaning bots producing measurably more misinformation than left-leaning ones.

A companion study published in Nature around the same period replicated the core finding across the United States, Canada, and Poland using 2024 and 2025 election data, lending cross-national validity to the British results.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns considering conversational AI for voter contact should recognize that the persuasion gains are real but tightly coupled to accuracy losses -- deploying chatbots without rigorous fact-checking infrastructure risks producing effective messaging that is also systematically misleading.
Sources: Science (doi:10.1126/science.aea3884), 2026; Nature, 2026; Scientific American, 2026

Spencer Pratt's AI-Fueled Run for LA Mayor

The former reality television personality whose Palisades home burned in last year's wildfires has turned a grief-driven mayoral bid into the most online-native campaign in American politics. Spencer Pratt has surged from 10 percent to 22 percent in recent polls, closing in on City Council President Nithya Raman and leaving incumbent Karen Bass increasingly vulnerable in a fractured field.

The campaign's engine is synthetic media. An AI-generated video by filmmaker Charles Curran depicting Pratt as Batman descending on a burning Los Angeles went viral across platforms, and Pratt amplified it without hesitation. His operation relies on an army of freelance video clippers who chop confrontational clips into short-form social content designed to dominate algorithmic feeds. The tone is deliberately abrasive -- Bass is branded with crude nicknames, and the rhetorical register targets young men in the manosphere.

Political scientists are divided on whether the approach represents a viable path to City Hall or merely a new ceiling for attention-based politics. What is not in dispute: Pratt has demonstrated that AI-generated content, influencer distribution tactics, and post-ironic provocation can move real poll numbers in a major American city. Trump himself has endorsed Pratt, calling the campaign a model for how online energy translates into political support.

Sources: NPR, 21 May 2026; CNN, 16 May 2026; Time, 21 May 2026; NBC News, 2026

Trump Orders Banks to Weigh Immigration Status as Financial Risk

President Trump signed an executive order on Tuesday titled "Restoring Integrity to America's Financial System" that directs federal financial regulators to issue guidance treating a customer's immigration status as a factor in assessing financial risk. The order instructs the Treasury Department to propose changes under the Bank Secrecy Act that would enable banks to collect immigration status and employment authorization data from customers.

The White House frames the order as a safety-and-soundness measure, arguing that lending to individuals without legal work authorization creates a structural ability-to-repay deficiency. Critics from the banking industry, civil rights organizations, and consumer advocates warn the order could effectively debank millions of people -- including U.S. citizens who lack passports or other citizenship documents, disproportionately affecting elderly Americans, low-income households, and rural residents.

The order represents the administration's latest effort to extend immigration enforcement into civilian institutions. It follows a pattern that includes enlisting local police departments, schools, and now financial institutions as enforcement checkpoints. Finance industry leaders had largely opposed the proposal during the comment period, warning that the data collection requirements would be extraordinarily costly to implement.

Sources: Time, 20 May 2026; NBC News, 20 May 2026; Bloomberg, 19 May 2026; American Banker, 2026

NC Swing Voters: Frustrated with Trump, Not Ready for Democrats

NPR observed two online focus groups this week featuring 12 North Carolina voters who supported Biden in 2020 and then switched to Trump in 2024 -- precisely the swing-voter archetype that will determine whether Democrats can reclaim the House in November. The sessions, conducted by Engagious and Sago as part of the ongoing Swing Voter Project, revealed a striking emotional paradox.

Half of the participants said they disapprove of the job Trump is doing. The economic frustration is acute: voters described feeling that the president is out of touch with the financial pain ordinary Americans face as the Iran conflict continues to push gasoline above four dollars a gallon. Several participants volunteered that they regret aspects of their 2024 vote.

Yet not a single participant said they planned to vote for Democrats in the midterms as a corrective. Seven identified as independents, four as Republicans, and one as a Democrat -- but party affiliation proved less predictive than cultural identity. Observers noted that support for Trump and the Republican brand appears remarkably sticky even among voters who acknowledge policy disappointment.

The finding carries direct implications for Democratic midterm strategy: converting economic frustration into ticket-switching may require a far more aggressive persuasion operation than the party has mounted so far. Passive dissatisfaction with the incumbent does not automatically produce opposition votes -- a lesson Democrats learned in 2022 when similar sentiment failed to translate into the anticipated red wave for Republicans.

Sources: NPR, 21 May 2026; Engagious/Sago Swing Voter Project, May 2026

Intelligence vs. Commerce: The AI Regulation Knife Fight

The Trump administration is sharply divided over who should govern the evaluation of frontier AI models, with the debate described by participants as a bureaucratic knife fight between the intelligence community and the Commerce Department. At stake is whether civilian regulators or spy agencies will serve as the primary gatekeepers for AI technology that could reshape both the economy and national security.

The White House Office of the National Cyber Director has proposed creating a large evaluation center within the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, which would give the intelligence community a significant new role in reviewing AI models before public release. Commerce Department officials are pushing back, arguing that their existing AI Safety Institute -- which already conducts voluntary evaluations through agreements with companies including OpenAI and Anthropic -- should retain primacy.

The catalyst for the turf war is Anthropic's Mythos model, whose cyber-vulnerability identification capabilities alarmed national security officials enough to shift the debate from abstract governance questions to urgent operational ones. The NSA is already testing Mythos under classified conditions. Rep. Jim Himes, the ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, publicly backed intelligence agency access at a Politico summit this month, though he argued Commerce should retain a parallel role.

The outcome will shape not just AI policy but the relationship between Silicon Valley and the security state for a generation. A forthcoming executive order is expected to establish a voluntary information-sharing framework, but the question of institutional primacy remains unresolved.

Sources: Washington Post, 11 May 2026; Nextgov/FCW, 12 May 2026; Lawfare, May 2026; Defense One, May 2026

America's AI Regulation Patchwork Has No Good Test for Any of Them

As of March 2026, state lawmakers in 45 states have introduced 1,561 AI-related bills covering everything from algorithmic accountability and generative-AI regulation to laws targeting nonconsensual deepfakes. At least 145 became law in 2025 alone. The result is a thicket of contradictory requirements that define core terms differently from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.

A hiring tool that satisfies California's four-year recordkeeping and anti-bias testing requirements must separately meet Colorado's impact assessment mandates and New York City's independent bias audit regime with its own distinct notice rules. The compliance burden has already driven corporate relocations: Palantir moved its principal executive office to Miami in February, and OpenAI signaled it would exit California amid state attorney general investigations.

The Trump administration's December executive order directed the DOJ to challenge state AI laws and conditioned broadband funding on alignment with a minimally burdensome national standard. But the 2026 NDAA, signed the day before, excluded preemption language entirely -- leaving the federal government demanding uniformity it has no statutory authority to enforce.

Iowa Enacts the Midwest's First Chatbot Safety Law

Governor Kim Reynolds signed Senate File 2417 on May 3, creating the first Midwestern framework governing public conversational AI services. The bill passed both chambers without a single dissenting vote -- a rare display of unanimity on technology regulation in a deeply partisan era.

The law's most consequential provisions target minors: AI operators must disclose the chatbot's non-human nature at session start and every three hours, random engagement rewards designed to keep minors online are banned, and protocols must redirect self-harm prompts to crisis hotlines. Chatbots are prohibited from posing as licensed therapists. For adults, visible disclosure remains mandatory whenever a reasonable user might believe the chatbot is human.

Operators that ignore the requirements face injunctive orders and civil penalties up to $500,000 per violation. The compliance deadline is July 1, 2027, giving operators fourteen months to adapt. The law arrives as Iowa becomes a test case for how conservative states can regulate AI without triggering the preemption battles that have consumed coastal legislatures.

Sources: GovTech, May 2026; AI CERTs News, May 2026; Radio Iowa, 15 Apr 2026

Trump's Afrikaner Refugee Preference Draws Legal Fire

The Trump administration has proposed raising the refugee admissions ceiling for white South African Afrikaners to as many as 17,500 for fiscal year 2026 -- more than double the administration's earlier total cap of 7,500 refugees from all countries combined. Between October and April, the United States resettled just over 6,000 refugees. All but three were from South Africa.

The racial dimension is now the subject of active litigation. Plaintiffs filed an amended complaint on April 8 challenging the administration's refugee determination, including provisions that explicitly prioritize Afrikaner admissions. The International Refugee Assistance Project argues the policy constitutes discriminatory preference based on race and national origin, noting that the government has granted exceptions to its broader refugee ban for over 3,000 white Afrikaners while refusing equivalent exceptions for refugees of other backgrounds.

The administration maintains that Afrikaners face genuine persecution in South Africa and qualify as victims of unjust discrimination. Critics counter that the policy's effect -- a 94 percent reduction in total refugee admissions paired with a racial carve-out -- reveals priorities that cannot be reconciled with either humanitarian law or the equal protection principles embedded in the Refugee Act.

$10.8 Billion: The Most Expensive Midterms in History

AdImpact projects that total political advertising spending in the 2026 midterm cycle will reach $10.8 billion, shattering previous records and cementing the midterms as the most expensive non-presidential election in American history. The most dramatic shift is in distribution: $2.5 billion is expected to flow to connected television and streaming platforms alone -- a 20 percent increase from the 2022 cycle and a signal that the industry's center of gravity has permanently migrated from linear broadcast.

Connected TV offers campaigns precise demographic and geographic targeting capabilities, real-time optimization of creative and placement, and lower spending minimums that make sophisticated advertising accessible to down-ballot candidates who were previously priced out. Most 2026 campaigns are now treating CTV as their primary television channel rather than a supplement to broadcast buys.

The battleground math is driving the spending surge. Democrats need a net gain of three House seats to win a majority, while Republicans can lose no more than two and retain control. Nine Senate seats are classified as toss-ups, with Democrats defending two and Republicans defending seven. The combination of competitive geography and new advertising technology is producing a spending environment where even state legislative races in key districts are attracting six-figure media budgets.

Sources: AdImpact via Basis, 2026; Campaigns & Elections, 2026; NBC News, 2026

Who Should Govern AI -- the States or Washington?

The Case for State Action

Progressive policymakers and consumer advocates argue that states must fill the vacuum created by federal inaction. With no comprehensive AI legislation on the congressional calendar and the Trump administration opposing mandatory regulation, state legislatures are the only institutions actively writing rules for algorithmic accountability, deepfake disclosure, and AI-in-hiring fairness.

Forty-five states have introduced AI bills in 2026 because their constituents face real-world harms that Washington has declined to address. Waiting for a federal framework that may never arrive leaves voters, workers, and consumers unprotected. State-level experimentation also provides a testing ground for policies that may eventually inform national standards.

Sources: Fortune, 15 May 2026; Transparency Coalition, 15 May 2026; Brookings, 2026

The Case for Federal Preemption

Industry leaders and free-market advocates warn that the state-by-state patchwork is crushing innovation and handing strategic advantage to China. With 1,561 bills defining core terms like "artificial intelligence" and "high-risk system" differently across jurisdictions, compliance costs are forcing companies to choose between operating in fewer states or spending millions navigating incompatible legal regimes.

Palantir, SpaceX, and OpenAI have all cited state regulatory burdens in corporate relocation decisions. A single, minimally burdensome national standard would preserve American competitiveness while providing the clarity that both developers and consumers need. Fragmentation is not democracy -- it is dysfunction.

Sources: Fortune, 30 Jan 2026; Vorys, May 2026; MultiState, 2026
