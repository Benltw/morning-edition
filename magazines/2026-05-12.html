Chatbot Voting Advice Applications Inform but Seldom Sway Young Unaligned Voters
Munzert S., Bail C.A., Budak C., Guess A.M., Nagler J., Tucker J.A.

Can an AI chatbot change your vote? A large-scale experimental study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences tested chatbot-based voting advice applications on young, politically unaligned citizens across multiple democracies. The researchers deployed conversational AI systems that guided users through issue-based questionnaires, matched their preferences to party platforms, and presented personalized recommendations. The central finding is sobering for anyone betting on AI-driven voter persuasion: chatbot voting advice applications reliably increased users' factual knowledge about party positions and improved their ability to identify which party best matched their stated preferences, but they produced minimal effects on actual voting intentions. Young unaligned voters — precisely the demographic that campaigns most covet — proved resistant to shifting their intended vote based on chatbot recommendations alone. The study suggests that information provision and persuasion operate through fundamentally different mechanisms, and that AI tools optimized for the former should not be expected to deliver the latter.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns deploying AI chatbots for voter outreach should calibrate expectations: these tools are effective at educating and informing, but unlikely to convert undecided voters on their own. The value lies in engagement and data collection, not direct persuasion.
Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), 2026 · doi: 10.1073/pnas.2515516122

Spy Agencies vs. Commerce: Who Controls America’s AI Regulatory Architecture?

A turf war inside the Trump administration is reshaping the future of American AI governance. According to a Washington Post investigation published May 11, U.S. intelligence agencies are demanding a central role in regulating advanced AI models, challenging the Department of Commerce's traditional lead on technology policy. The fight was triggered by the national security implications of Anthropic's Mythos model, which demonstrated unprecedented capabilities in identifying and exploiting cybersecurity vulnerabilities. The National Security Agency is already using Mythos, even as the broader administration maintains Anthropic's designation as a supply chain risk following the company's refusal to grant the Pentagon unrestricted access to its technology. The White House is now weighing an executive order that would formalize government vetting of all new frontier AI models before market release — a process the West Wing and National Security Council want completed within weeks. The irony is inescapable: an administration that entered office promising to dismantle Biden-era AI oversight is now building a surveillance architecture for AI that goes further than anything its predecessor attempted, driven not by precautionary principle but by the raw logic of national security competition.

Connecticut Enacts SB 5: The Most Comprehensive State AI Law in America

Connecticut's Artificial Intelligence Responsibility and Transparency Act has cleared both chambers and awaits Governor Lamont's signature, which his office has confirmed is forthcoming. SB 5 spans 64 pages and 37 sections, covering terrain no other state law has attempted in a single bill: frontier model safety requirements for any developer training models above 10^26 floating-point operations, mandatory whistleblower protections for employees who flag catastrophic risks, safety regulations for emotional AI companion chatbots marketed to minors, transparency obligations for synthetic content, and a new framework governing AI in automated hiring pipelines. The law defines a "large frontier developer" as any company exceeding $500 million in annual revenue — a threshold that captures every major AI lab. Implementation begins October 1, 2026, with AI companion protections for minors taking effect January 1, 2027. The bill passed the House 131 to 17 and the Senate 32 to 4, reflecting an unusually broad bipartisan consensus in a year where federal AI legislation remains stalled. For the campaign technology community, the whistleblower protections are particularly significant: they create a legal shield for AI safety researchers inside companies whose tools are increasingly deployed in electoral contexts.

States Considering Laws to Ban Legal Personhood for AI

The Question No One Expected to Reach the Statehouse Floor

Should an AI model be prosecuted for breaking the law? Can an artificial intelligence system serve as a corporate officer, own property, or hold contractual rights? These once-theoretical questions have arrived at state legislatures across America. NPR reported on May 11 that at least seven states, led by Ohio, are actively considering legislation to ban any form of legal personhood for AI systems. Ohio state representative Thaddeus Claggett's bill goes further than most, prohibiting even limited legal status — blocking AI from serving as corporate managers, holding property, or entering contracts in its own capacity. The bills represent a preemptive strike: no U.S. jurisdiction has granted AI legal personhood, but legislators are responding to academic proposals, corporate lobbying for AI agent autonomy, and the rapid deployment of AI systems that operate with increasing independence. Supporters argue that only humans should bear legal responsibility. Critics counter that blocking all legal status for AI could complicate liability frameworks precisely when clear accountability is most needed. Claggett has expressed hope that enough states will act to push Congress toward a nationwide ban.

Supreme Court Hands GOP Another Redistricting Win; Democrats Still Lead House Forecast

Ongoing: redistricting battle — new development. The 6–3 ruling follows the Court's April 29 decision in Louisiana v. Callais, which weakened a key provision of the Voting Rights Act and invalidated a majority-Black, Democrat-held district in Louisiana. Trump’s AI Pirouette: From “Anything Goes” to Defense Production Act Oversight

Ongoing: Federal AI governance — policy reversal

The Trump administration's approach to artificial intelligence has undergone a transformation that its own officials struggle to explain without irony. Eighteen months ago, the White House positioned itself as the antithesis of Biden-era AI caution, dismissing safety testing requirements as innovation-killing bureaucracy and signing executive orders designed to accelerate deployment across every sector. Now, driven by the national security shock of Anthropic's Mythos model, the same administration is invoking the Defense Production Act to compel companies training the largest AI models to share safety testing results with the federal government.

The reversal accelerated in early May. CNBC reported on May 5 that the administration will test models from Google, Microsoft, and xAI under the new framework — the first time a Republican White House has mandated pre-release government review of private AI systems. Axios described the shift as a policy driven by genuine alarm: Mythos's ability to identify and exploit cybersecurity vulnerabilities at machine speed forced officials to confront the possibility that deregulation could leave the nation exposed. The White House is now developing guidance to allow agencies to bypass Anthropic's supply chain risk designation and onboard Mythos itself, while simultaneously building the regulatory architecture to prevent the next Mythos from arriving without warning. For the political technology community, the implications are direct: if frontier model vetting becomes federal policy, every AI tool used in campaigns will operate under a new compliance landscape by 2027.

AI-Generated Campaign Ads Go Mainstream Across 2026 Midterms

The ads range from AI-narrated attack spots using synthetic voices to entirely generated video sequences depicting policy scenarios. Twenty-nine states have either banned political deepfakes or required disclosure of AI-generated content in campaign materials, but the patchwork of laws leaves significant gaps — particularly around AI-assisted content that falls short of full deepfake generation. Vermont became the latest state to pass legislation addressing synthetic media in elections, but the regulatory response remains slower than the technology. Experts warn that the 2026 cycle represents a threshold: AI-generated political content is no longer an edge case to be flagged but a baseline capability available to any campaign with a modest budget.

Parasocial Politics: How Social Media Bonds Drive Gen Z Voting Intentions

A new study published in Telematics and Informatics Reports examines the growing role of parasocial relationships in political behavior — the one-sided psychological bonds that followers develop with political figures through social media. The research finds that politicians who share personal content, respond to comments, and maintain consistent digital presence create the illusion of genuine personal relationships with followers, and that these parasocial bonds are now a measurable predictor of electronic word-of-mouth sharing and voting intention. The finding aligns with Affective Intelligence Theory, which identifies enthusiasm as a key driver of voter mobilization: parasocial relationships generate precisely the kind of emotional engagement that activates political participation. The 2026 landscape confirms the theory in practice. Social media is now the primary news source for 67 percent of Gen Z and 61 percent of millennials, according to Reuters Institute data. Politicians from Narendra Modi to Donald Trump reach hundreds of millions through direct social feeds. And the success of candidates like Zohran Mamdani, whose multilingual social media strategy helped win New York City's Democratic mayoral primary, demonstrates that parasocial politics is no longer a supplement to traditional campaigning — it is the campaign.

The 64% Divide: Republican Consultants Embrace AI While Democrats Lag Behind

A partisan gap in AI adoption has opened inside the political consulting industry — and it may already be shaping the 2026 campaign landscape. The American Association of Political Campaign Consultants' latest survey found that 64 percent of Republican consultants report using AI tools daily in their campaign work, compared to 49 percent of Democrats. The gap is not about access or cost — both parties have equivalent tools available — but about institutional culture and risk tolerance. Republican firms have moved aggressively to integrate AI into ad production, opposition research, voter micro-targeting, and fundraising copy, treating the technology as a competitive advantage to be exploited at speed. Democratic firms, shaped by a party culture more sensitive to the ethical and regulatory concerns surrounding AI, have adopted more cautiously, often requiring additional review layers before deploying AI-generated content. The irony is that the party most invested in regulating AI is slower to use it, while the party most opposed to regulation is furthest ahead in deployment. Whether the Republican adoption advantage translates into measurable electoral gains remains an open question — but in a cycle where margins are razor-thin, a fifteen-point gap in daily AI usage is not trivial.

Should States Ban Legal Personhood for AI?

The Case for Banning AI Personhood

Legal personhood has always been reserved for entities capable of moral reasoning, social obligation, and accountability. Granting any form of legal status to AI systems — whether as corporate officers, property holders, or contractual agents — would erode the foundational principle that humans bear responsibility for the tools they create. Ohio's proposed legislation reflects a concern shared by civil liberties organizations and labor advocates: if AI systems can hold legal status, companies may use that status to diffuse liability, making it harder to hold human decision-makers accountable when AI causes harm. The risk is especially acute in electoral contexts, where AI-driven systems already operate with significant autonomy in voter outreach, ad targeting, and content generation. Preemptive legislation ensures that as these systems grow more capable, the legal framework remains unambiguously human-centered.

Framing drawn from: NPR, Connecticut Public, Ohio General Assembly, civil liberties commentary

The Case Against a Blanket Ban

Blocking all legal status for AI may feel prudent, but it risks creating more problems than it solves. As AI systems increasingly operate as autonomous agents — executing contracts, managing portfolios, making procurement decisions — the absence of any legal framework for their actions creates a liability vacuum. If an AI agent causes financial harm and cannot be held legally accountable in any capacity, the injured party may have no clear path to redress. Technology industry representatives and some legal scholars argue that limited, carefully defined legal status for AI agents could actually strengthen accountability by creating clear chains of responsibility. A blanket ban, they contend, could push AI deployment into jurisdictions with no guardrails at all, or force companies to create elaborate workarounds that obscure rather than clarify who is responsible when things go wrong.

Framing drawn from: technology industry commentary, legal scholarship, liability analysis

Solidarity Tech → All-in-One Progressive Organizing CRM

It consolidates calling, texting, email outreach, website management, event coordination, and donor tracking into a single CRM — replacing the fragmented stack of five or six separate tools that most progressive campaigns have historically maintained. The platform is designed to mobilize voters, engage supporters, manage volunteers, and run data-driven field campaigns with real-time performance reporting.


Who is using it: Solidarity Tech has been adopted by progressive campaigns, union organizing drives, and grassroots advocacy groups in the 2026 cycle. The platform positions itself as purpose-built for the organizing model that progressive campaigns favor — relationship-based, volunteer-intensive, and community-rooted — in contrast to the data-brokerage model that dominates Republican political tech. Its integration of calling and texting with CRM data allows organizers to track individual voter journeys from first contact through election day.


What outcome has been reported: Campaigns using the platform report significant reductions in tool-switching overhead and improved volunteer retention, driven by a simplified interface that lowers the technical barrier for non-professional organizers. The real-time reporting layer gives campaign managers immediate visibility into contact rates, event attendance, and donation conversion, enabling tactical adjustments that previously required end-of-week data pulls from multiple vendors.
