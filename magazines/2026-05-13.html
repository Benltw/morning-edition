Attitude Manipulation and Voting Intentions
Nicolai Berk · ETH Zurich / Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin

Does changing someone’s mind about a policy change how they vote? A representative survey experiment fielded in Germany (N = 3,004) and published in Political Behavior in 2026 tested this assumption head-on. Berk exposed respondents to persuasive framing on salient policy issues and measured whether the resulting attitude shifts translated into changes in vote intention. The central finding challenges a core assumption of political campaigning: while citizens’ policy attitudes proved highly responsive to framing effects, that responsiveness did not carry over into voting intentions. Attitudinal change failed to move the electoral needle regardless of how strongly respondents identified with a given party. The study draws on both rational choice and identity-based theories of voting behavior to explain the disconnect, concluding that cue-taking is a more conscious and deliberate process than much of the literature assumes. Voters appear to compartmentalize — updating their views on an issue without updating their candidate preference. The implication is stark: persuasion campaigns that succeed in shifting policy opinion may be measuring the wrong outcome if their goal is electoral conversion.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns investing in issue-framing should not assume that moving the polling needle on a policy question will move votes. Persuasion and mobilization require fundamentally different strategies — and the former cannot substitute for the latter.
Political Behavior, 2026 · Springer Nature · doi: 10.1007/s11109-025-10035-5

AI Algorithms Supercharge the Redistricting Arms Race on Both Sides

Ongoing: redistricting battle — new development on how the maps are actually drawn. Both Republican and Democratic redistricting operations are now deploying artificial intelligence algorithms capable of generating and evaluating millions of possible congressional maps for a single state. A Time investigation published May 11 details how tools like the Algorithm-Assisted Redistricting Methodology (ALARM) Project use Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling to produce thousands of legally compliant maps at scale, enabling statisticians to benchmark any proposed map against a massive neutral baseline. The technology cuts both ways: Republican operatives in Texas used algorithmic optimization to implement a mid-decade redistrict five years early, potentially flipping five seats before November. Democrats and voting rights groups, meanwhile, deploy the same algorithms in court, arguing that extreme statistical outliers in proposed maps prove intentional partisan gerrymandering. Experts caution that AI does not simply produce a “perfect map” on command — human choices about which legal criteria to optimize remain decisive. But the sheer computational power changes the game: where a human analyst might evaluate dozens of configurations, an algorithm explores trillions. The result is a redistricting landscape in which both sides wield extraordinary technical firepower, and the courts must increasingly adjudicate disputes between dueling algorithmic claims. With the Supreme Court having weakened Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act in recent months, AI-generated map analyses are likely to become the primary evidentiary tool in the fights ahead.

Time, 11 May 2026 · Democracy Docket · Cook Political Report · Yale Insights

Inflation Hits a Three-Year High as the Iran War Reshapes the Midterm Landscape

The Consumer Price Index rose 0.6 percent in April and 3.8 percent over the past twelve months — the sharpest annual increase since 2023 — driven overwhelmingly by the Iran conflict’s stranglehold on global energy markets. Energy costs surged 3.8 percent month-over-month and 17.9 percent year-over-year. Brent crude is trading at $105 a barrel, up 44 percent since hostilities began, and average gasoline prices have climbed more than one dollar per gallon. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed for ten weeks, choking off roughly one-fifth of the world’s oil supply. The political fallout is immediate: eight in ten Americans now say gas prices are straining household budgets, and 63 percent blame the president, according to the latest Marist poll. The European Central Bank has warned that a prolonged conflict could push Germany and Italy into technical recession by year-end. For campaigns, the math is punishing — affordability was already the top midterm issue before April’s CPI print, and every tenth of a point of additional inflation compounds voter discontent. The Federal Reserve faces its own bind: rate cuts remain off the table just as the president’s preferred nominee prepares to take the central bank’s helm.

AI Industry Super PACs Flood 2026 Races with $100 Million — on Everything Except AI

The artificial intelligence industry is spending at an unprecedented scale to shape the 2026 midterms, but its ads are conspicuously silent about artificial intelligence. NBC News reports that competing super PACs backed by AI executives — including Leading the Future, funded by OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman, Palantir’s Joe Lonsdale, and Andreessen Horowitz — have banked over $100 million for this cycle. Instead of arguing for deregulation directly, the groups are running ads about immigration, health care, and Trump-era controversies, mirroring a long-standing influence playbook: win the seat first, shape the policy later. Leading the Future operates through two connected entities, Think Big (backing Democrats) and American Mission (backing Republicans), hedging its bets across party lines. On the opposing side, Public First — which has received at least $20 million from Anthropic — has pledged $50 million to back pro-regulation candidates from both parties. The result is a proxy war fought on terrain that voters actually care about, while the real stakes — whether the federal government imposes meaningful guardrails on frontier AI models — remain largely invisible to the electorate. An NBC/YouGov poll finds that a majority of voters believe the risks of AI outweigh the benefits, yet few are aware of the scale of industry spending aimed at shaping who will decide those rules.

West Virginia Fires the Starting Gun: Capito and Anderson Win First 2026 Primaries

The 2026 midterm election season officially began Tuesday evening in West Virginia, where Republican Senator Shelley Moore Capito cruised to her party’s nomination within minutes of polls closing at 7:30 p.m. Capito, seeking a third term with President Trump’s endorsement, defeated five challengers, including state Senator Tom Willis, who campaigned against career politicians. On the Democratic side, Rachel Fetty Anderson emerged from a five-candidate field that included Jeff Kessler and Zach Shrewsbury. West Virginia is deep-red territory — Trump carried it by 39 points in 2024 — and Capito is considered a prohibitive favorite in November. But the primary offers early data points for analysts watching turnout, enthusiasm gaps, and the performance of Trump-endorsed candidates heading into a cycle where the president’s 37 percent approval rating weighs on Republican prospects nationwide. The state’s gubernatorial primary also drew attention, with the results setting the stage for what will be the most closely watched midterm cycle in a generation.

Global Elections

Lebanon Postpones Elections as War Upends the Democratic Calendar

Beirut

Lebanon’s parliament has voted to postpone general elections by two years, pushing the next vote to 2028 and leaving the country without a fresh democratic mandate as it struggles to rebuild from the 2026 Hezbollah–Israel war. The elections had been scheduled for early May, with expatriate voting set for May 1 and 3 and in-country polling on May 10. The postponement reflects a convergence of crises: ongoing military operations have displaced hundreds of thousands, electoral infrastructure in southern Lebanon has been destroyed, and deep disagreements persist over whether to allow the full diaspora to vote for all 128 parliamentary seats rather than just six. Hezbollah, weakened militarily and financially by the conflict, opposed any changes to the electoral law that could further erode its political footprint. The decision is a blow to the reform movement that emerged after the 2019 uprising and gained parliamentary seats in 2022, as well as to the government of Prime Minister Nawaf Salam and President Joseph Aoun, whose election was itself seen as a sign of Hezbollah’s diminished influence. The Atlantic Council notes that Hezbollah retains strong support among its core constituency, even as its capacity to project power across Lebanese politics has contracted sharply. For the broader region, the postponement raises questions about the credibility of democratic processes under wartime pressure and the willingness of entrenched actors to submit to the ballot when the outcome is uncertain.

The Voter Roll Crisis: Higher Ground Labs Says Political Tech Must Become Democracy Tech

Attorney General Pam Bondi has requested complete voter registration records from Minnesota, including Social Security numbers and driver’s license data, as part of a federal push that has now triggered DOJ lawsuits against at least 23 states and the District of Columbia. Higher Ground Labs, the leading political technology incubator for the progressive ecosystem, is warning that this is not just a legal fight but a technological one. In a new series on election disruption, the organization argues that federal databases are rife with historical inaccuracies, and cross-referencing voter rolls against them would generate an avalanche of false positives — flagging legitimate voters with similar names and creating chaos for campaigns, election officials, and individual citizens trying to exercise their rights. The deeper concern is surveillance: the data requests would enable the federal government to monitor political participation, cross-reference individuals across databases, and identify people for intimidation or targeted enforcement. Higher Ground Labs concludes that the political technology community can no longer treat campaign efficiency and democratic resilience as separate problems. Data sovereignty, end-to-end encryption, and distributed architectures are no longer aspirational features — they are operational requirements for any platform that touches voter data in an environment of active government hostility toward civic participation.

Colombia’s Three-Way Race Enters the Final Stretch Before May 31

Ongoing: Colombia presidential election — updated polling. With 18 days until the first round, Colombia’s presidential race has consolidated into a three-candidate contest. Pacto Histórico senator Iván Cepeda holds a commanding lead at 44.3 percent in the latest Invamer survey of 3,800 voters, trailed by attorney Abelardo De la Espriella at 21.5 percent and Centro Democrático senator Paloma Valencia at 19.8 percent. The story beneath the topline is Valencia’s surge: she has nearly doubled her share from 10 percent in February, positioning herself as the establishment uribista alternative and threatening to overtake De la Espriella as the candidate best positioned for a runoff. Former Bogotá mayor Claudia López has collapsed from 11.7 to 3.6 percent, and former Medellín mayor Sergio Fajardo is at just 2.5 percent. If no candidate clears 50 percent on May 31, a runoff between the top two is scheduled for June 21. The calculus matters: AtlasIntel puts De la Espriella nine points ahead of Valencia, but Guarumo and Invamer show them within striking distance. If Valencia overtakes De la Espriella in the next round of polls, analysts at AS/COA suggest the runoff becomes a genuine coin-flip rather than a comfortable Cepeda victory. The race is being closely watched as a bellwether for the Latin American left after a cycle of pendulum swings.

$10.8 Billion: The Most Expensive Midterms in American History

AdImpact projects total political advertising spending in the 2026 midterms will reach $10.8 billion, a 21 percent increase over the $8.9 billion spent during the 2022 cycle and a new record for any non-presidential election year. The growth is being driven by two converging forces: the explosion of Connected TV, where political spending is projected at $2.48 billion (up 20 percent from 2024), and the compression of the traditional advertising calendar, with campaigns spending earlier and shifting budgets faster in response to real-time events. Political data vendors like L2, TargetSmart, and Data Trust have improved their CTV matching capabilities to 50–70 percent for clean voter files, making the medium increasingly attractive for micro-targeted persuasion. At least 15 campaign ads featuring AI-generated content have run since November, and the need for more content at scale is accelerating AI video production by campaigns, primarily for opposition messaging through Super PACs. The sheer volume of spending is creating a new problem: voter fatigue. OpenSecrets reports that campaigns are now competing not just against each other but against the ambient noise of an electorate saturated with political messaging six months before Election Day.

A Majority of Voters Now Say the Risks of AI Outweigh the Benefits

An NBC News/YouGov poll finds that a majority of American voters believe the risks of artificial intelligence outweigh its benefits — a finding that sits awkwardly alongside the AI industry’s record spending to influence the elections in which those voters will participate. The poll captures a growing gap between elite enthusiasm for AI and public wariness: while 82 percent of state CIOs report that government employees are using generative AI daily, and 64 percent of Republican consultants say they use AI tools in their campaign work, ordinary voters see a technology that threatens jobs, produces unreliable information, and operates without meaningful accountability. The disconnect presents a strategic dilemma for candidates. Running on AI innovation risks alienating anxious voters; running against it risks alienating donors, particularly the super PACs now spending nine figures on midterm races. The polling also reveals a partisan wrinkle: Republican voters are more likely to see AI as a net positive, while Democrats are more skeptical — a mirror image of the consultant divide, where GOP operatives lead in AI adoption. For campaigns navigating the 2026 landscape, the message is clear: talk about what AI does for people, not what AI is.

Who Should Regulate AI — Innovation Champions vs. Safety Advocates?

The Pro-Innovation Case: Let Markets Lead, Let America Win

Leading the Future and its allied super PACs — backed by Andreessen Horowitz, Palantir’s Joe Lonsdale, and OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman — argue that premature regulation will cede America’s AI lead to China. Their position: a light-touch national framework should preempt the patchwork of state laws now emerging, preserve innovation incentives, and let market competition sort winners from losers. President Trump’s executive order banning state-level AI regulation aligns with this view, and the $100 million in super PAC spending reflects a conviction that the 2026 midterms will determine whether the regulatory window closes permanently. The group backs candidates from both parties who support this framework, signaling that the fight is about policy outcomes, not partisan allegiance.

NOTUS · NBC News · Built In · CNBC, Jan 2026

The Safety-First Case: Guardrails Before Scale

Public First — supported by at least $20 million from Anthropic and aligned with safety-focused researchers — contends that self-regulation has failed and that frontier AI models pose systemic risks requiring independent oversight before deployment, not after. They point to Connecticut’s SB 5 as a model: whistleblower protections, transparency mandates, and safety evaluations for the most powerful systems. Their candidates, also drawn from both parties, argue that states have a legitimate role in protecting citizens when the federal government moves too slowly. Critics of the deregulatory approach, including some MAGA-aligned populists, note that blocking state laws strips communities of self-governance and benefits a Silicon Valley elite that has historically ignored working-class concerns.

Public First · Popular Information · Texas Tribune · Future Caucus

$ quiller — AI Fundraising Copilot for Progressive Campaigns
What it does: Quiller is a generative AI platform purpose-built for political fundraising and engagement communications. Trained on thousands of high-performing fundraising emails and calibrated by industry experts, it generates first drafts of emails, SMS messages, press releases, op-eds, and digital ad copy — all customized to match a candidate’s voice, tone, and urgency level. The platform is designed to compress the content production cycle from hours to minutes, freeing campaign staff to focus on strategy rather than copywriting.

Who is using it: Quiller is deployed exclusively by Democratic and progressive campaigns and organizations across the 2026 cycle. The company works closely with early partners to refine its models and explicitly restricts access to the progressive ecosystem — it will not sell to Republican campaigns or conservative organizations.

What outcome has been reported: Campaigns report that Quiller reduces email drafting time by up to 80 percent while maintaining or improving open and conversion rates compared to human-written copy. The platform’s custom approach to urgency calibration — matching tone to the fundraising moment rather than defaulting to perpetual alarm — has been cited as a key differentiator in an era of donor fatigue.
> takeaway: Quiller represents the maturation of AI campaign tools from novelty to operational necessity. The partisan restriction is notable — it mirrors the broader ecosystem split where progressive and conservative campaigns increasingly inhabit separate technology stacks. As AI-generated content becomes indistinguishable from human writing, the competitive advantage shifts from who can produce content to who can produce the right content at the right moment.
