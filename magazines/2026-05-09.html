Attitude Manipulation and Voting Intentions

Political Behavior · Springer Nature · 2026

Persuasive political communication is effective at shifting people's policy attitudes — but those shifts do not translate into changed voting intentions. That is the central finding of a new study published in Political Behavior, which tested whether experimentally induced changes in issue positions lead voters to update their party preferences. The researchers drew on both rational choice and identity-based theories of voting, hypothesizing that citizens would resolve the cognitive dissonance created by a widening gap between their manipulated attitude and their party's position by adjusting either their vote or their belief. The results were unambiguous: voters overwhelmingly adjusted their issue positions back toward their preferred party rather than changing their vote. This held regardless of the strength of respondents' partisan attachment, contradicting a mechanistic model in which policy preferences drive electoral choice. The finding underscores the dominance of partisan identity over policy reasoning in democratic elections.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns investing heavily in issue-based persuasion may be overstating its downstream electoral value. Partisan identity, not policy attitude, appears to be the binding constraint on voter behavior — suggesting that mobilization and identity-reinforcement strategies may outperform persuasion-first approaches.

Source: Political Behavior, Springer Nature, 2026. doi:10.1007/s11109-025-10035-5

Russia-Ukraine Three-Day Ceasefire Takes Effect on Victory Day

A three-day ceasefire brokered by President Trump took effect on Friday as Russia marked its annual Victory Day military parade in Moscow. Both Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov confirmed the arrangement, which suspends all kinetic military activity from May 9 through May 11 and includes a prisoner exchange of one thousand from each side. Zelenskyy accompanied his confirmation with a pointed decree stating that Ukrainian weapons would not target Red Square during the parade — a calculated piece of strategic communication that simultaneously acknowledged Ukraine's strike capability and signaled restraint as a diplomatic gesture. The ceasefire followed days of competing unilateral pause announcements: Russia had declared a two-day halt for Victory Day, while Ukraine proposed its own terms. Trump's intervention merged the two into a mutually agreed framework. Whether the three-day window leads to broader negotiations remains deeply uncertain, but it represents the first jointly accepted pause since the full-scale invasion began in February 2022. Both sides have used previous unilateral ceasefires to reposition forces, and analysts warn that the same dynamic could undermine this one.

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, CBC News, Bloomberg, Euronews — May 8-9, 2026

Reform Takes 1,350 Seats, Starmer Defiant as Leadership Challenges Loom

Ongoing: The full count from Thursday's UK local elections confirms a political earthquake. Reform UK gained more than 1,350 council seats and took control of 13 councils, winning 27 percent of the national equivalent vote — the largest single-party share. Labour lost over 1,300 councillors and 35 councils, including Wigan, which the party had controlled for more than fifty years, where Reform won all twenty seats it contested. In Hartlepool, Reform swept all twelve available seats. Labour also lost its century-long majority in Wales. Prime Minister Keir Starmer insisted on Friday that he will not resign, but dissent is mounting within his own ranks. Labour MP Jonathan Brash said publicly that Starmer should not survive these results. Speculation has turned to potential challengers including Health Secretary Wes Streeting, former Deputy Prime Minister Angela Rayner, and Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham. The Green Party also made significant gains, adding 259 seats and winning control of three councils. Multiple analysts now doubt Starmer will lead Labour into the next general election, which must be held by 2029.

Sources: BBC News, ITV News, CNN, Al Jazeera, The Irish Times, Bloomberg — May 8-9, 2026

Virginia Supreme Court Blocks Democratic Redistricting Map

The Virginia Supreme Court ruled 4-3 on Friday that the state's Democratic-led legislature violated procedural requirements when it placed a mid-decade redistricting amendment on the ballot. The core issue was timing: the General Assembly voted on October 31 to propose the constitutional amendment, but by that date more than 1.3 million Virginians — roughly 40 percent of the electorate — had already cast early ballots. The court held that Virginia's "general election" includes the early voting period, not just Election Day itself, meaning the proposal came too late. The ruling overturns voters' approval of a new congressional map that would have given Democrats an advantage in ten of eleven House districts. Instead, the existing 6-5 Republican-favoring map remains in force for the November midterms. The decision deals a significant blow to Democrats' strategy of counter-redistricting to offset Republican gerrymandering in states like Tennessee, Florida, and Louisiana. Republicans celebrated the ruling as a defense of procedural norms; Democrats called it a partisan outcome from a court whose four-justice majority was appointed by Republican governors. The case also establishes a new precedent about the relationship between early voting and legislative action deadlines that could reverberate well beyond Virginia.

Sources: NBC News, NPR, Virginia Mercury, The Hill, Axios Richmond, CBS News — May 8, 2026

Trump White House policy reversal on AI regulation

From “Anything Goes” to Government Vetting of Frontier AI Models

The Trump administration has executed a dramatic pivot on artificial intelligence oversight, abandoning its deregulatory stance in favor of pre-deployment government evaluation of frontier AI models. The catalyst was Anthropic's Mythos Preview, which discovered exploitable vulnerabilities in every major operating system and web browser upon its limited release to a handful of American companies including Apple, Amazon, JPMorgan Chase, and Palo Alto Networks. National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett compared the proposed framework to FDA drug approval, telling Fox Business that future AI models with security implications should go through a government review process before public release. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation has since struck agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and Elon Musk's xAI allowing the federal government to evaluate models before they reach the market. The White House also rejected a plan to expand Mythos access to 70 additional companies, keeping the tool tightly restricted. A Washington Post investigation described the episode as a secretive AI tool that is fracturing Trump's inner circle, with some advisors embracing the new oversight posture while others warn it contradicts the administration's founding promise to the technology sector.

Sources: Fortune, CNBC, The Washington Post, The Register, The Hill — May 5-8, 2026

EU Rewrites Its Own AI Act: High-Risk Deadlines Pushed to 2027

The EU Council presidency and European Parliament reached a provisional agreement on Wednesday to simplify and streamline the AI Act — the world's most ambitious artificial intelligence regulation — just months before its toughest provisions were due to take effect. The revisions, part of the broader Omnibus VII simplification package, push the compliance deadline for standalone high-risk AI systems from August 2026 to December 2027, and to August 2028 for AI embedded in products — up to sixteen months of additional preparation time for industry. Small and medium-sized enterprise exemptions, including simplified technical documentation requirements, now extend to companies with up to 500 employees. The agreement also introduces new prohibitions against AI-generated non-consensual sexual content and child sexual abuse material. Regulatory sandboxes at the national level are delayed to August 2027. On the enforcement side, transparency obligations for AI-generated content are actually tightened, with the deadline shortened from six months to three months, landing on December 2, 2026. The agreement now awaits formal adoption by both institutions before August. For political technology operators in Europe, the message is mixed: more time to comply on the hardest requirements, but faster-moving deadlines on transparency and labeling.

Sources: EU Council, European Parliament, IAPP, Lewis Silkin, EU News — May 7, 2026

One in Eight Lawmakers Won't Return: Record Congressional Retirements

The exodus from Congress is reshaping the competitive landscape six months before voters head to the polls

More than one in eight members of Congress have announced they will not seek reelection in November, setting the second-highest departure rate in the last century and opening dozens of seats to competitive contests in a cycle already defined by turbulence. In the Senate, fourteen members are retiring or running for different offices — seven Republicans, four Democrats, and three seeking other positions. The Republican departures include Mitch McConnell, the longest-serving Senate leader in history, along with Thom Tillis and Joni Ernst, all of whom have clashed at times with President Trump's vision of expanded executive power. On the Democratic side, Dick Durbin and Jeanne Shaheen are among those passing the torch to a younger generation. The wave has created open-seat races in states that were not originally expected to be competitive, complicating both parties' electoral math. For Republicans, the retirements compound an already difficult environment: Trump's approval ratings sit at their lowest point of his second term, the generic congressional ballot shows Democrats leading by roughly ten points, and the enthusiasm gap between the two parties' voters stands at fourteen points. Democrats enter the cycle with structural advantages in motivation but face redistricting losses that could offset a national wave. The sheer volume of open seats — with no incumbent advantage to protect them — means the midterm map is more fluid and unpredictable than at any point since the 2006 wave that swept Democrats into power.

Trump DOJ Pushes Voter Roll Purges Using Error-Prone Federal Database

The Justice Department has launched a sweeping effort to obtain voter registration files from nearly every state and review them for suspected non-citizens using a federal immigration database called SAVE — the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements system. The initiative marks an unprecedented attempt by the federal government to insert itself directly into state-level voter roll maintenance, and critics say it is testing the limits of the National Voter Registration Act's 90-day quiet period, which prohibits systematic removal programs close to an election. The accuracy of the underlying tool is a central concern. When Idaho used SAVE to cross-reference its 1.1 million registered voters, the system initially flagged 760 as potential non-citizens — but after manual review, only about three dozen cases were referred to law enforcement. Similar false-positive rates have been reported in other states. The legal battleground is an Arizona case now before the Supreme Court that asks whether the NVRA's quiet period applies to the kind of federal-level review the DOJ is conducting. That case is unlikely to be resolved before November, but emergency litigation could force courts to intervene if purges are attempted weeks or days before the midterm vote. Voting rights organizations have called the effort a voter suppression tool disguised as election security.

Sources: CNN, Higher Ground Labs, Toda Peace Institute — May 2026

Colombia's Runoff Math Shifts as Valencia Nearly Doubles Support

Ongoing: With twenty-two days until Colombia's May 31 presidential first round, the race beneath Ivan Cepeda's commanding 44.3 percent lead is rapidly reconfiguring. Center-right senator Paloma Valencia of the Democratic Center has nearly doubled her support from 10 percent to 19.8 percent in a single polling cycle, closing the gap on second-place Abelardo de la Espriella at 21.5 percent. If Valencia overtakes de la Espriella and secures a runoff spot, a weighted polling average suggests the June 21 second round becomes a coin-flip race rather than the comfortable Pacto Historico victory most analysts had assumed. The consolidation of the left accelerated this week when former environmental candidate Francia Marquez Murillo dropped out and joined Cepeda's campaign. Former Bogota mayor Claudia Lopez has collapsed from 11.7 percent to 3.6 percent, her support scattering across multiple candidates. The question now is whether Cepeda can clear fifty percent on the first ballot — which would avoid a runoff entirely — or whether the Colombian right consolidates quickly enough behind a single challenger to force the issue into June.

Cyprus: 753 Candidates and 19 Parties Contest a Fragmenting Parliament

Cyprus heads to parliamentary elections on May 24 with the most crowded ballot in its democratic history: 753 candidates from 19 political parties, plus a cohort of independents, are competing for 56 seats in the House of Representatives. The top of the field is a dead heat: ruling DISY leads polling at 22.3 percent, barely ahead of opposition AKEL at 22.1 percent. The far-right ELAM party holds 13.8 percent, while the newest entrant, the reformist movement ALMA — founded in May 2025 by former Auditor General Odysseas Michaelides — has surged from zero to 10.2 percent in barely a year, running on a platform of systemic restructuring and political transparency. Traditional center party DIKO polls at 9.4 percent. The OSCE has deployed election observers for the vote. The fragmented field all but guarantees that no single party will approach a governing majority, setting up complex post-election coalition negotiations that could reshape Cypriot politics for a generation. The previous record for candidacies was 651 in 2021.

Government AI Moves From Sandbox to Operations

A survey by the National Association of State Chief Information Officers found that 82 percent of state CIOs now report their employees are using generative AI tools in daily work — a figure that would have been unthinkable eighteen months ago when most state governments were still running pilot programs. The 2026 GovTech 100 report highlights a market that has moved from testing new ideas to applying technology in ways that support real operational needs, with eight new companies recognized this year for innovations in AI, public safety, infrastructure, identity, and service delivery. For political technologists, the takeaway is that government AI adoption is no longer a question of whether but of how fast and at what price.

Should Governments Vet AI Models Before Public Release?

The Case for Pre-Release Government Oversight

Anthropic's Mythos demonstrated that a single AI model can discover exploitable vulnerabilities in every major operating system and browser. Proponents of government vetting argue that releasing such capabilities without prior review poses an unacceptable national security risk. The CAISI agreements with Google, Microsoft, and xAI represent a reasonable starting point: let government evaluators test frontier models before they reach the public, much as the FDA reviews drugs before they reach pharmacies. Without mandatory oversight, hostile actors could weaponize these tools before defenders have time to respond.

Sources: Fortune, CNBC, The Hill — May 2026

The Case Against Government Gatekeeping

Critics argue that an FDA-style approval regime for artificial intelligence would create bureaucratic bottlenecks that slow American innovation while competitors in China and elsewhere face no such constraints. The same administration that campaigned on deregulation and dismantled Biden-era AI safety initiatives is now proposing the kind of heavy-handed oversight it once ridiculed. Voluntary industry partnerships and market-driven safety incentives, not mandatory government vetting, are the appropriate mechanisms for managing risk without throttling the technology sector that drives American economic competitiveness and national strength.

Sources: The Register, The Washington Post, industry commentary — May 2026
