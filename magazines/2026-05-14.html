01 · Research Insights of the Day
Political Parasocial Relationships on Social Media: The Role of User Experiences and Emotions in Shaping eWOM and Voting Intention
Achmad Nizar Hidayanto, Hana Fitriani, Andi Afifah Khairunnisa, Kesya Aurelya, Muhammad Damar Kusumo, Steven

Social media has become the primary arena where political actors forge emotional bonds with voters they will never meet in person. This study, drawing on 565 valid survey responses analyzed through Structural Equation Modeling, finds that parasocial relationships formed on political social media accounts significantly shape both electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) and voting intention. The researchers applied the PAD framework, measuring pleasure, arousal, and dominance as emotional mediators between social media experience and political behavior.

The key finding is striking: pleasure is the strongest mediator. When users experience homophily (perceived similarity) and relational intimacy with a political figure online, the resulting pleasure drives them to share political content and, ultimately, to vote for that figure. Arousal and dominance play secondary but significant roles. The study was conducted in the context of the Indonesian 2024 presidential election, but the mechanism has clear cross-cultural implications for any polity where social media is a dominant campaign channel.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns that invest in creating perceived similarity and relational warmth on social media, rather than mere information delivery, may generate stronger word-of-mouth effects and ultimately higher vote share. The emotional pathway matters more than the informational one.
Telematics and Informatics Reports / ScienceDirect, 2025 · doi.org/10.1016/S2772-5030(25)00087-8

Ongoing: Redistricting

South Carolina Senate Derails Trump's 7-0 Map; McMaster Expected to Call Special Session

The Republican-led South Carolina Senate voted 29-17 on Tuesday to reject a measure that would have allowed lawmakers to redraw the state's seven congressional districts after the legislative session ends today. Five Republican senators broke ranks to join all 12 Democrats in blocking the effort, which was backed personally by President Trump.

The proposal, championed in the House, would have replaced the current 6-1 Republican map with a 7-0 configuration. Representative Nancy Mace confirmed that the White House had provided state lawmakers with the proposed map. But the Senate's refusal may not be the final word: multiple sources told WIS-TV on Wednesday that Governor Henry McMaster is expected to call lawmakers back for a special session on redistricting, keeping the fight alive past today's sine die deadline.

The standoff illustrates the tension between Trump's desire to maximize Republican seats and state-level Republican legislators who worry about legal exposure and the political cost of eliminating the state's sole Democratic district, currently held by Jim Clyburn's successor.

04 · 2026 Primaries

Louisiana's Saturday Showdown: Three Republicans, One Senate Seat, and Trump's Long Memory

On Saturday, Louisiana Republicans will choose among incumbent Senator Bill Cassidy, Trump-endorsed Representative Julia Letlow, and State Treasurer John Fleming in a primary that has become a referendum on loyalty to the president. The latest Emerson College poll shows a virtual three-way tie: Fleming at 28%, Letlow at 27%, and Cassidy at 21%. If no candidate wins an outright majority, a runoff follows on June 27.

Cassidy's original sin, in MAGA terms, was his 2021 vote to convict Trump of incitement of insurrection. Trump endorsed Letlow on January 18 in what amounts to a grudge match five years in the making. Cassidy has attempted to run on his legislative record, including infrastructure wins for the state, but in deep-red Louisiana, the endorsement deficit may prove insurmountable.

The race is one of three Republican primaries this month that will test whether Trump's grip on the party has loosened amid falling approval ratings, internal MAGA splits over the Iran war, and the Epstein files controversy. The other two: Kentucky's 4th District (May 19) and a contested House seat in Georgia.

Al Jazeera, Emerson College Polling, Christian Science Monitor, NBC News · May 13, 2026
05 · AI in Politics

Kentucky's $25 Million Cage Match: Deepfakes, AIPAC Money, and the Most Expensive House Primary in History

The Republican primary in Kentucky's 4th District between Representative Thomas Massie and Trump-endorsed challenger Ed Gallrein has become, by a wide margin, the most expensive U.S. House primary ever. Ad spending has surpassed $25.6 million according to AdImpact, with the May 19 vote still five days away.

The race has also become a live laboratory for AI-generated political attacks. A super PAC called MAGA KY, funded largely by hedge fund manager Paul Singer and casino magnate Miriam Adelson, aired an AI deepfake video depicting Massie dining with Representatives Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Ilhan Omar. Massie denounced the ad as defamatory. A rival PAC responded with its own AI-fabricated video showing Gallrein fleeing from Trump on a fictional battlefield. Both ads may violate a 2025 Kentucky law requiring disclosure of AI in political advertising.

Massie has said that at least 95 percent of the funding opposing him comes from pro-Israel lobbying groups. AIPAC's United Democracy Project has spent $2.6 million backing Gallrein, and the Republican Jewish Coalition has added $4 million more. The race has become a proxy war over U.S. foreign policy, isolationism, and the boundaries of AI-powered campaign warfare.

Axios, LPM/WVXU, OECD.AI, Middle East Eye, WHAS11 · May 5-14, 2026

07 · Political Technology

Silicon Sampling: Pollsters Are Replacing Humans with AI Bots — and the Industry Is Divided

Synthesized from New Statesman, Pew Research Center, Nate Silver Bulletin · May 2026

YouGov acquired Yabble, a New Zealand-founded synthetic responses pioneer, in 2024. Savanta launched Virtual Personas, letting clients access on-demand AI focus groups representative of target audiences. And Aaru, the billion-dollar New York start-up, now simulates entire electorates using multi-agent AI, generating thousands of demographically profiled bots that can be polled on candidate favorability, issue salience, or message resonance in hours rather than weeks. The age of silicon sampling has arrived in political polling, and it is dividing the industry.

Proponents argue the approach is faster, cheaper, and free from the nonresponse bias that plagues traditional surveys. Aaru's platform accurately predicted the 2024 U.S. election outcome with a margin of fewer than 400 votes, using 5,000 AI agents. Republican campaigns have been early adopters: the National Republican Senatorial Committee is using AI to create ads, analyze data, and identify new small donors, while GOP strategists simulate voter attitudes toward events like the Iran war in near-real time.

Critics are not persuaded. Nate Silver has called AI polls simply fake polls. A May 12 Pew Research Center analysis warned that AI-generated and bogus respondents threaten polling's future by contaminating the signal that democracy depends on. Campaigns and Elections reported new research showing how AI bots can manipulate online polls outright. And a quiet transparency scandal has already surfaced: a statistic from a simulated Aaru survey was included in an Axios article without initially being labeled as synthetic, sparking criticism about undisclosed methodology.

The stakes go beyond polling accuracy. If synthetic respondents replace real ones at scale, democratic representation risks becoming a feedback loop: AI trained on historical data producing outputs that reinforce existing patterns rather than capturing genuine shifts in public opinion. For campaign strategists and policymakers alike, the question is not whether silicon sampling will spread, but whether the industry can build norms of transparency and validation before the technology outpaces its governance.

New Statesman, Pew Research Center (May 12), Silver Bulletin, Campaigns & Elections, Semafor · May 2026
08 · AI & Democracy

MIT's Three-Layer Blueprint for Using AI to Strengthen Democracy

A major essay published May 5 in MIT Technology Review argues that AI is becoming the primary interface through which citizens form beliefs and participate in self-governance, and that without deliberate design, it will erode the public sphere rather than enhance it. The authors propose a three-layered framework.

At the informational layer, AI companies must ensure model outputs are truthful. A recent field evaluation found that people across political viewpoints rated AI-written fact-checking notes as more helpful than human-written ones, suggesting AI-assisted verification can achieve cross-partisan credibility.

At the agentic layer, AI assistants must represent their users faithfully without reinforcing motivated reasoning. The essay warns that a public sphere where everyone has a personalized agent attuned to their existing views would become a collection of private worlds inhospitable to shared deliberation.

At the institutional layer, several U.S. states and localities are already using AI-mediated platforms for democratic deliberation at scale. Research shows AI mediators can help citizens find common ground. The essay calls this the most promising frontier: AI not as a threat to democracy but as infrastructure for its next iteration.

MIT Technology Review · May 5, 2026
09 · GovTech

From Sandbox to Scale: Nearly 90% of Federal Agencies Now Deploy AI in Operations

A Google Public Sector survey released this month found that nearly 90% of U.S. federal agencies are now using AI in some operational capacity, up from scattered pilot programs just two years ago. The shift from experimentation to production is accelerating across traffic optimization, fraud detection, permitting automation, and citizen-facing services.

But the GovTech 100 report published by Government Technology magazine underscores persistent obstacles. Only 11% of surveyed government organizations have deployed agentic AI systems in production. Legacy system integration, data architecture constraints, and inadequate governance frameworks remain blockers. Security and adversarial risk are the single biggest concern, cited by 48% of all agencies.

The global AI-in-government market is projected to expand from $22.4 billion in 2024 to over $98 billion by 2033. Deloitte's 2026 GovTech Trends report emphasizes that the next frontier is not adoption but accountability: proving to legislators and skeptical residents that these deployments actually improve outcomes. The era of AI demos in government is over. The era of AI results has begun.

Google Cloud Blog, GovTech / Government Technology, Deloitte, AI Multiple · May 2026
10 · Political Campaigning

Progressives Bet AI Anxiety Will Win Them Seats in November

A growing cohort of progressive candidates is making AI regulation a centerpiece of their 2026 campaigns, calculating that voter anxiety about job displacement, deepfakes, and surveillance can be converted into ballot-box support. NOTUS reports that Democrats in swing districts are explicitly running on plans to rein in the AI industry, framing the issue as a kitchen-table concern rather than a tech-policy abstraction.

The strategy has financial headwinds. AI industry groups have poured over $100 million into 2026 races, often backing candidates who favor lighter-touch regulation. But progressives counter that public sentiment is on their side: the latest polling shows a majority of voters believe AI's risks outweigh its benefits, and the affordability crisis linked to AI-driven automation fears has given the message new urgency.

Anthropic's $20 million donation to Public First Action, supporting candidates who favor AI guardrails, adds a novel wrinkle: a major AI company funding the very regulation its competitors oppose. Whether AI anxiety becomes a durable political identity, like climate or gun control, or fades into the background noise of the midterm cycle, may depend on whether progressives can make the abstract concrete before November.

NOTUS, Quartz, ABC News, Deseret News · May 2026

12 · Social Media Trends

TikTok's Algorithmic Bias: Nature Study Finds Disproportionate Pro-Republican Content Amplification

A study published in Nature found that TikTok's recommendation algorithm disproportionately exposed users to pro-Republican and anti-Democratic content in the months before the 2024 U.S. election. The researchers concluded that the bias exceeded what could be explained by engagement patterns alone, though they could not determine whether the amplification was intentional or an emergent property of the ranking system.

The findings have taken on new significance in 2026 as TikTok operates under its restructured U.S. entity, TikTok USDS, following the January divestiture deal. A new briefing paper from Oxford's Internet Institute, reported by Phys.org in February, outlines ongoing concerns around content moderation policies, political influence, and election integrity on the platform. GLAAD's 2026 Social Media Safety Index gave TikTok low marks for algorithmic transparency.

With the EU's Digital Services Act now fully active and imposing stricter transparency obligations on very large platforms, the pressure on TikTok to open its algorithmic ranking systems to independent researchers is mounting. For campaigns, the question is operational: if the algorithm systematically favors one party's content, does organic reach become an uneven playing field that paid advertising cannot fully correct?

14 · Political Tech Application

$ aaru --simulate-electorate

Aaru is a predictive simulation platform that recreates entire populations using multi-agent AI technology. Founded in New York, the company generates thousands of AI agents with detailed demographic profiles, media consumption patterns, and behavioral traits. These synthetic voters can be polled on candidate favorability, issue salience, and message resonance in hours rather than the weeks traditional surveys require.

Republican campaigns have been the earliest and most aggressive adopters. The National Republican Senatorial Committee uses Aaru-style tools to create ads, analyze data, and identify new small donors. GOP strategists simulate voter attitudes toward fast-moving events like the Iran war and scan social media for real-time opinion shifts. By contrast, Democratic operatives have been slower to adopt the technology, citing privacy concerns and workforce displacement fears.

Aaru completed a $50 million Series A at a $1 billion valuation in late 2025, with partners including Accenture, Ernst & Young, and IPG. Its 16-year-old CTO led the team that predicted the 2024 U.S. election with a margin of fewer than 400 votes using 5,000 AI agents.

> takeaway: Synthetic voter simulation is no longer a research experiment. It is operational campaign infrastructure, deployed at scale on one side of the aisle. The partisan adoption gap may itself become a strategic variable in November.

New Statesman, Semafor, Axios, 36Kr, RealClearMarkets · 2024-2026
