Rationality, Affect, and Vote Choice

Frontiers in Political Science, August 2025

Across five United States presidential elections spanning 2000 to 2016, researchers found that emotional reactions to candidates consistently outweigh rational policy evaluations in determining vote choice. Positive affect toward one candidate over another — the simple feeling of warmth, enthusiasm, or trust — exerted a larger effect on the final ballot decision than ideological alignment or issue-position matching. The study challenges the enduring assumption that voters are primarily rational actors who weigh platforms and then choose. Instead, the evidence suggests that campaigns succeed or fail on their ability to generate positive emotional associations — and that negative campaigning, while attention-grabbing, may be less decisive than cultivating genuine affective bonds with the electorate.

Practitioner implication: Campaign messaging strategies that prioritize evoking positive emotional responses — hope, pride, personal connection — may yield stronger vote-share gains than negative attacks or detailed policy comparisons alone.

Frontiers in Political Science, published 18 August 2025; also covered by PsyPost.

Hungary's Magyar Sworn In, Races to Unlock €18B in Frozen EU Funds

Péter Magyar was sworn in as Hungary's Prime Minister on May 11, ending Viktor Orbán's sixteen-year rule and opening the most ambitious democratic reconstruction project in Central Europe since 1989. His Tisza party holds a constitutional supermajority — 141 of 199 seats — won on the largest voter turnout in Hungary's democratic history. Magyar's first-week agenda is punishing: unblock approximately €18 billion in EU funds frozen over rule-of-law failures by meeting 27 Commission milestones before an August deadline; propose a two-term limit for prime ministers; join the European Public Prosecutor's Office to signal anti-corruption credibility; and call on Orbán-era appointees — including the president and the head of the Constitutional Court — to resign. The Commission confirmed talks have begun, and Magyar has said rule-of-law reforms could be in place by month's end. The window is narrow: funds not unlocked by late summer are lost permanently.

Record 38 Republicans Flee the House Ahead of November

The exodus is now the largest since modern recordkeeping began in 1930. As of mid-May, 57 House members have announced they will not seek reelection — 36 Republicans and 23 Democrats — with a record 38 GOP members departing their seats. In the Senate, eleven senators are stepping aside, including Mitch McConnell, Thom Tillis, and Joni Ernst. The Hill reports that House Republicans are "fleeing Congress in record numbers amid growing dysfunction," with an unusually high number running for governor or Senate rather than defending increasingly vulnerable seats. The political math is straightforward: open seats are harder to hold, and a wave of retirements in a cycle already tilting toward Democrats compounds the structural challenge. In 2006 — the last blue-wave cycle of comparable scale — only 17 Republicans retired from the House. Today, that figure has more than doubled.

EU Digital Fairness Act Targets “Addictive Design” on TikTok and Instagram

The European Commission announced on May 12 that it will regulate "addictive design" features — endless scrolling, autoplay, push notifications, and algorithmic recommendation loops — that steer children toward harmful content on major platforms. The proposed Digital Fairness Act, expected before the end of 2026, builds on the Digital Services Act with strict limits on AI-driven engagement mechanics, a ban on addictive design techniques targeting minors, and new requirements for children's privacy and online security. The Commission has already found Meta in breach of the DSA for failing to keep under-13s off its platforms, and issued preliminary findings in February that TikTok's design itself — not just its content — violates EU law. President von der Leyen confirmed that an age-verification app modeled on the EU COVID Certificate is in development, and member states will be able to integrate it into digital wallets. Australia's under-16 social media ban, enacted in December, is accelerating the global trend.

MIT's Three-Layer Blueprint for Using AI to Strengthen Democracy

Political Technology | AI Governance

A framework published in MIT Technology Review on May 5 proposes that AI's impact on democracy operates across three distinct layers — and that policymakers must address all three simultaneously or risk losing democratic legitimacy to technological drift. The first layer is epistemic: how citizens come to know things. Here, the authors argue AI companies must ensure model outputs are truthful, and that the informational environment doesn't collapse into algorithmically reinforced silos. The second layer is agentic: as people increasingly delegate civic actions to AI — from drafting public comments to scheduling votes — agents must faithfully represent users without developing agendas of their own, a technically daunting requirement in domains where preferences are ambiguous. The third layer is institutional: policymakers should harness AI's capacity to make governance itself more responsive, building on early experiments in AI-mediated deliberation platforms that help citizens find common ground at scale. Several U.S. states and localities are already running such platforms. The framework's provocation is that these three transformations — in how we know, how we act, and how we govern — are not future risks but present realities unfolding without coordinated democratic oversight.

Sources: MIT Technology Review, 5 May 2026; MIT GOV/LAB; Carnegie Endowment, Jan 2026

The decision echoes a broader pattern — from Myanmar to Libya — where post-conflict states default to postponement over the uncertainties of competitive elections.

AI Chatbots on the Campaign Trail: Wisconsin's 1.1 Million Voter Experiment

Before Wisconsin's April 7 Supreme Court election, the Defend Our Courts PAC deployed an AI-powered "virtual assistant" built by startup Convos to text 1.1 million voters identified as undecided — generating more than 10,000 two-way conversations in under three weeks. The chatbot pulled responses exclusively from candidate Chris Taylor's website, judicial record, past speeches, and news coverage — no generative hallucination, no large-language-model improvisation. Research published in Nature and by Cornell confirms the stakes: AI chatbots move opposition voters' preferences by 10 percentage points or more, roughly four times the measured effect of traditional political advertising. The Wisconsin deployment is the largest documented use of conversational AI for direct voter persuasion in a U.S. judicial race. A UW-Madison professor called the practice a "slippery slope." No federal regulation governs AI-to-voter communications; Utah alone requires disclosure.
