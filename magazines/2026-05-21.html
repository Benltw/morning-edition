Systematic Partisan Content Skews in TikTok During the 2024 US Elections

Talal Rahwan & Yasir Zaki -- Nature, 6 May 2026

An audit study published in Nature deployed 323 bot accounts across three US states to track TikTok's recommendation algorithm over 27 weeks of the 2024 presidential campaign. The accounts were conditioned to mimic partisan viewing habits, with some watching pro-Democratic content and others pro-Republican content. After analyzing more than 280,000 recommended videos through a combination of human review and AI classification, the researchers found a statistically significant asymmetry: accounts seeded with Republican-leaning content received 11.5 percent more co-partisan material than their Democratic counterparts.

Perhaps more striking, Democratic-leaning accounts were shown significantly more anti-Democratic content than Republican accounts were shown anti-Republican content, suggesting a structural tilt in how the platform's recommendation engine surfaces oppositional material. The study was confined to three states and does not claim to establish a causal link to voting behavior, but it provides the most rigorous observational evidence to date that TikTok's algorithm did not treat the two partisan camps symmetrically.

Practitioner implication: Campaign digital strategists should audit platform-specific algorithmic exposure before allocating organic content budgets -- a dollar spent on TikTok may reach partisan audiences unevenly, making A/B testing across platforms essential rather than optional.
Source: Nature (doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10447-1), 6 May 2026

Trump Signs AI Executive Order: 90-Day Pre-Launch Review for Frontier Models

The White House is expected to issue an executive order as early as today establishing a voluntary framework under which AI developers would give the federal government early access to frontier models before public release. The order calls for a 90-day pre-launch review window, though some companies have pushed for a shorter 14-day period.

The executive order also outlines cybersecurity hardening requirements around advanced AI infrastructure. It represents a notable reversal from the administration's earlier posture of minimal AI regulation, though the voluntary nature of the framework means enforcement will depend on industry cooperation rather than statutory mandate.

Colorado Democrats Censure Gov. Polis Over Tina Peters Commutation

The Colorado Democratic Party voted overwhelmingly to formally censure Governor Jared Polis on Wednesday evening, with 90 percent of the State Central Committee backing the resolution. The move -- extraordinary for a sitting governor from his own party -- condemns Polis's decision to commute the prison sentence of Tina Peters, the former Mesa County clerk convicted of tampering with voting machines in an effort to prove 2020 election fraud.

Peters was originally sentenced to nearly nine years in prison. Polis reduced the sentence to four years and four-and-a-half months, with parole effective June 1. The party resolution agreed with the Republican district attorney who prosecuted the case that the commutation was a gross injustice, and suspended the term-limited governor from speaking at or attending Democratic Party events as a featured guest.

Polis has maintained that outside pressure had nothing to do with his decision, insisting Peters's original sentence was disproportionate. But the resolution pointedly stated that reducing the sentence under pressure from Donald Trump sends a message that election tampering has consequences only if the offender lacks powerful friends.

Sources: CPR News, 20 May 2026; Colorado Newsline, 20 May 2026

Illinois Democrats Unveil Eight-Bill AI Regulation Package

With no federal AI legislation in sight and the clock ticking toward a May 31 adjournment, Illinois Senate Democrats introduced an ambitious eight-bill package on May 13 that would make the state one of the most comprehensively regulated AI environments in the country. The bills tackle consumer protections, chatbot transparency, developer accountability, and the use of artificial intelligence in public schools.

Senate Bill 315, sponsored by Senator Mary Edly-Allen, targets developers with annual gross revenues exceeding $500 million, requiring them to publish frameworks detailing how they incorporate industry safety standards, assess model capabilities, evaluate catastrophic risk, and respond to safety incidents. The bill is explicitly modeled on California and New York proposals, with sponsors aiming to create a de facto national standard through state-level action.

Education receives particular attention. Senate Bill 416 from Senator Robert Martwick would prohibit teachers from using AI to assign grades and direct school boards to adopt AI usage policies by the 2026-27 academic year. A companion bill from Senator Karina Villa would restrict schools from deploying facial recognition software on campus cameras.

A separate proposal by Senator Steve Stadelman would ban bots from purchasing tickets for concerts, sports events, and shows -- a provision that received unanimous Senate support. The package reflects a broader trend of Democratic-led states filling a regulatory vacuum that Washington shows no sign of addressing before the midterms.

Sources: Capitol News Illinois, 14 May 2026; Chicago Sun-Times, 12 May 2026

Barney Frank, 1940 -- 2026

Barney Frank, the quick-witted Massachusetts congressman who became one of the first openly gay members of Congress and co-authored the most sweeping financial reform since the New Deal, died Wednesday at age 86. He had entered hospice care at his home in Maine last month.

Elected to the House in 1980, Frank served 16 terms representing the Bay State's 4th district. In 1987, during his fourth term, he voluntarily came out as gay -- a decision that was rare for its era and carried substantial political risk. In 2012, he became the first sitting member of Congress to marry a same-sex partner, wedding Jim Ready in a ceremony officiated by Governor Deval Patrick.

His legislative legacy centers on the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, which he steered through the House as chairman of the Financial Services Committee in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis. The law established new liquidity requirements and stress tests for banks, created the Volcker Rule limiting risky proprietary trading, and founded the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Frank was known as much for his rhetorical sharpness as for his policy reach. A relentless debater who rarely lost an exchange on the House floor, he combined progressive conviction with a pragmatist's willingness to negotiate. His death closes a chapter in both LGBTQ political history and the ongoing debate over how to regulate American finance.

Sources: NBC News, 20 May 2026; Axios, 20 May 2026; CNN, 20 May 2026

House Democrats Eye 2027 Federal Crackdown on AI Campaign Ads

Representative Joe Morelle, the ranking Democrat on the House Administration Committee, told Axios that regulating AI-generated political advertising will be a top legislative priority if Democrats retake the chamber in November. Morelle has been meeting with AI companies about a bill that would mandate disclosure when political ads use synthetic media.

The push follows an explosion of AI-generated content in the 2026 midterms. The National Republican Senatorial Committee produced deepfake videos of Senator Chuck Schumer and Texas state Representative James Talarico, while a Kentucky primary ad used AI-generated imagery to fabricate scenes involving Representative Thomas Massie. Currently, 28 states have some form of AI-in-elections law, but none have been tested in court and most are inconsistently written.

At the federal level, the Trump administration has signaled opposition to mandatory AI regulation, making prospects for bipartisan legislation dim in the current Congress. The Democratic strategy appears to treat the issue as a 2027 priority contingent on winning the midterms, framing it as a voter-protection measure rather than a technology-restriction agenda.

DOJ Indicts Prosecutor Who Disguised Jack Smith Files as Cake Recipe

Carmen Mercedes Lineberger, a 62-year-old former federal prosecutor in Port St. Lucie, Florida, was indicted on charges of theft of government property, destruction or falsification of records, and concealment of public records after allegedly emailing sealed portions of Special Counsel Jack Smith's classified-documents report to her personal Hotmail account.

The method was notably low-tech for a case involving the highest levels of federal law enforcement: Lineberger allegedly saved excerpts of internal DOJ communications and memoranda in a file named "Chocolate cake recipe.pdf" on her government-issued computer, then emailed the file to herself with the subject line "chocolate cake recipe." The indictment was unsealed on May 20.

Lineberger pleaded not guilty at an arraignment in West Palm Beach. The case is being investigated by the FBI and the Justice Department's Office of the Inspector General. It adds another chapter to the long-running saga of the Smith investigation and its political aftershocks.

AI Deepfakes in Political Ads: Regulate or Protect Free Speech?

The Case for Regulation

Democrats and election-integrity watchdogs argue that unregulated AI deepfakes in campaign advertising pose an existential threat to informed democratic participation. With no federal disclosure requirement, voters cannot distinguish authentic video from synthetic fabrication. The 2026 midterms have already produced deepfakes depicting real candidates in fabricated scenarios.

Advocates point to mandatory disclosure as a minimal, speech-compatible intervention: voters simply need to know when content is AI-generated. Twenty-eight states have acted precisely because federal inaction left a vacuum.

Sources: Axios, 20 May 2026; American Prospect, 17 Apr 2026; OECD AI Incident Monitor, Mar 2026

The Case for Caution

Republican strategists and free-speech advocates warn that deepfake regulation risks becoming a tool for censoring legitimate political expression. AI-generated content, they argue, is a form of political speech protected by the First Amendment -- and mandatory labeling could chill innovation in campaign communications at precisely the moment campaigns need new tools to reach disengaged voters.

Some state-level deepfake laws have already faced constitutional challenges on free-speech grounds. The Trump administration has signaled firm opposition to mandatory AI regulation of any kind.

Sources: CNN, 13 Mar 2026; MultiState, 12 Feb 2026; Bangor Daily News, 16 Apr 2026
