Research Insights of the Day

Politicians’ Theories of Voting Behavior

Lucas, J., Sheffer, L., Loewen, P.J., Walgrave, S. & Soontjens, K. · American Political Science Review · Vol. 119, No. 3 · 2025

Using face-to-face interviews with nearly one thousand politicians across eleven countries and corresponding surveys of more than twelve thousand citizens, the authors reveal a striking disconnect: politicians overwhelmingly hold thin, minimalist, ‘democratic realist’ theories of voting — they believe citizens vote on party loyalty, personality, and vague impressions rather than policy substance. Citizens, by contrast, hold more optimistic and policy-oriented theories of their own behavior. This misalignment is remarkably consistent across all eleven countries studied, suggesting it is structural rather than cultural. The findings imply that politicians systematically underestimate their electorates’ capacity for issue-based reasoning, which in turn shapes how they campaign, communicate, and ultimately represent their constituents.

Practitioner implication: If politicians are wrong about why people vote — if citizens are more policy-driven than elites assume — then campaigns that default to personality-driven messaging may be leaving persuasion on the table. The paper suggests that campaigns should test policy-rich messaging more aggressively rather than assuming voters only respond to character and affect.

Source: Cambridge University Press · APSR · 2025

Political Campaigning · Ongoing: Trump Retribution Primaries

Defy Trump, Lose Your Seat: Indiana Delivers a Warning to the GOP

The results are in, and Trump’s retribution campaign has succeeded. Five of the seven Republican state senators who voted against Trump’s preferred congressional redistricting map lost their primaries Tuesday night to Trump-endorsed challengers, backed by roughly $12 million in television advertising from Trump-aligned groups. State Sen. Linda Rogers fell to anesthesiologist Brian Schmutzler. State Sen. Dan Dernulc was unseated by insurance broker Trevor De Vries. State Rep. Greg Walker lost to state Rep. Michelle Davis. Only Greg Goode survived clearly; Spencer Deery’s race against Trump-backed Paula Copenhaver was separated by just three votes and may head to a recount. The message to every Republican legislator in America is now unambiguous: cross the president on redistricting — or anything else — and your career will be terminated in a primary. For strategists, the Indiana results confirm that Trump’s endorsement power remains devastating in low-turnout state races, precisely the terrain where most redistricting battles are fought. The downstream effect on 2028 map-drawing will be profound: no Republican state legislator will dare resist a White House redistricting directive again.

Sources: NBC News · NPR · Washington Post

Political Campaigning · Ongoing: Iran War

Trump Pauses Project Freedom After One Day, Claims Iran Deal Progress

In a stunning about-face, President Trump announced Tuesday evening that he is pausing ‘Project Freedom’ — the U.S. military operation to guide commercial ships through the Strait of Hormuz — just 24 hours after it launched under a hail of Iranian fire. Trump cited ‘Great Progress’ toward a ‘Complete and Final Agreement’ with Iran and said the operation would be paused ‘for a short period of time.’ The reversal came hours after the administration had framed the mission as a life-or-death necessity, with Secretary Rubio stating that at least ten sailors had died under Iran’s blockade. The U.S. blockade of Iranian ports remains in place. Diplomatic attention now shifts to Wednesday, when Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi is expected to visit Beijing, where the U.S. hopes China will pressure Tehran to release its chokehold on the strait. For campaign operatives, the whiplash creates a messaging problem: Republicans spent Monday rallying behind a muscular military operation only to see it pulled back Tuesday. Democrats will frame the reversal as chaotic; Trump allies will call it dealmaking genius. The truth may not matter — what matters is whether a deal materializes before November.

Sources: CNBC · CNN · Al Jazeera · NBC News

Ohio Sets Up the Cycle’s Marquee Races: Ramaswamy vs. Acton, Brown vs. Husted

Tuesday’s Ohio primaries produced two blockbuster general-election matchups. Vivek Ramaswamy, the 40-year-old former presidential candidate, won the Republican gubernatorial nomination after spending $25 million of his own money on top of $25 million raised from donors — setting up what will likely be the most expensive governor’s race in Ohio history. He faces Democrat Amy Acton, the state’s former health director who became a household name during the pandemic. Cook Political Report has already shifted the race from ‘Likely R’ to ‘Lean R.’ In the Senate special election, former Sen. Sherrod Brown, 73, won the Democratic nomination in his comeback bid against Sen. Jon Husted, turning what was expected to be a safe Republican hold into a genuine toss-up. For national strategists, Ohio’s twin races represent a test of whether the Rust Belt has permanently realigned rightward or whether the right Democratic candidates can still compete in a state Trump won by eight points.

California’s Seven-Way Governor Debate Exposes a Party in Search of Itself

Seven candidates — five Democrats and two Republicans — took the CNN debate stage Tuesday night at East Los Angeles College, each scrambling to distinguish themselves with ballots already arriving in voters’ mailboxes. The two-hour clash exposed deep fractures in both parties. Asked for one word to describe Gavin Newsom’s legacy, Antonio Villaraigosa said ‘Performative,’ Katie Porter said ‘Bold,’ Tom Steyer said ‘Progressive,’ and Xavier Becerra said ‘Game-changing’ — revealing a party that cannot agree on whether its recent past was a success or a cautionary tale. On the Republican side, Steve Hilton and Chad Bianco both branded Newsom a ‘Failure,’ but sparred over California’s high-speed rail project, with Hilton defending infrastructure investment while Bianco called it government waste. For campaign professionals watching from other states, the California debate offers a preview of what post-Biden Democratic primaries will look like nationally: crowded fields, ideological confusion, and no consensus on whether to run toward or away from the progressive era.

Global Elections · Ongoing: Colombia Presidential Race

Colombia’s Left Surges to 44% With 26 Days to Go

With Colombia’s presidential election just 26 days away on May 31, progressive senator Iván Cepeda has consolidated a commanding lead at 44.3 percent in the latest Invamer survey of 3,800 voters — up sharply from 37.1 percent in February. Attorney Abelardo De la Espriella trails at 21.5 percent, while Centro Democrático senator Paloma Valencia has nearly doubled her support to 19.8 percent, emerging as the fastest-rising candidate. The political centre has collapsed entirely: former Bogotá mayor Claudia López plunged from 11.7 to 3.6 percent, and Sergio Fajardo from 6.6 to 2.5. A runoff on June 21 between the top two finishers remains the base case, as no candidate is likely to clear fifty percent. Cepeda proposes a National Constituent Assembly, unconditional dialogue with armed groups, and greater state control over the economy — a more radical agenda than Gustavo Petro’s 2022 campaign. His stance on the U.S. military operation that removed Nicolás Maduro from power in Venezuela — he called it ‘kidnapping’ — has already drawn warnings from Washington. For hemisphere-focused strategists, a Cepeda presidency would dramatically reshape the U.S.-Colombia relationship at a moment when Trump has simultaneously escalated Cuba sanctions and seeks an Iran deal requiring regional allies’ cooperation.

Sources: Rio Times · AS/COA · Americas Quarterly

Political Technology · Ongoing: AI in Campaigns

AI Chatbots Are Now Persuading Voters at Scale — and No Law Stops Them

The era of AI-powered voter persuasion is no longer hypothetical — it is happening in live races. A Deseret News investigation published Sunday reveals that high-profile Senate campaigns are using AI to produce entire video ads, while AI chatbots trained on candidate talking points are engaging voters in personalised text-message conversations designed to shift their votes. In Wisconsin’s April Supreme Court election, the Defend Our Courts PAC deployed an AI chatbot called Convos to message more than 1.1 million voters, generating over 10,000 conversations in under three weeks — at a cost of just $84,000. Research published in Nature in December 2025 confirmed that LLM-driven conversations can shift voting intentions more effectively than traditional political advertising. Yet the regulatory landscape remains almost entirely vacant: while 31 states now regulate deepfake political ads, no state has passed a law governing AI chatbots that converse with voters in real time. The chatbots do not disclose they are AI unless asked, and unlike deepfake ads, they leave no public record that journalists or regulators can audit. A 64-to-49 percent gap between Republican and Democratic consultants using AI daily suggests the technology is already creating an asymmetric capability advantage.

Sources: Deseret News · WPR · Nature · Axios

AI in Politics · Ongoing: AI Regulation

Deepfake Laws Hit 31 States — But Courts Keep Striking Them Down

The regulatory landscape for AI-generated political content has reached a paradox: more states than ever have enacted deepfake disclosure laws — 31 as of this month, up from 28 at the start of the year, with Maine, Tennessee, and Vermont joining the list — yet the legal foundations of these laws are crumbling. A federal judge struck down California’s landmark deepfake statute as unconstitutionally broad, holding that it discriminated based on content when it could have been ‘narrowly tailored to aim at false speech that actually causes voter interference, coercion, or intimidation.’ Minnesota faces a similar challenge from X (formerly Twitter). Ballotpedia reports that 15 new deepfake bills have been enacted so far in 2026, but the number of states with deepfake laws hasn’t grown proportionally because some laws are being revised or replaced. Looking ahead, lawmakers are broadening their approach: rather than just punishing creators, new bills target AI platforms, payment processors, and distribution networks that enable synthetic political content at scale. For campaign compliance teams, the patchwork remains a nightmare — 31 different disclosure standards with no federal floor and active constitutional challenges in multiple circuits.

Sources: Public Citizen · Ballotpedia · MultiState

Apply AI Summit Brussels and IAPP Europe: Where EU AI Act Compliance Gets Real

With Rise of AI Berlin wrapping its tenth-anniversary edition this week, Europe’s AI governance calendar shifts to two events that will define how the EU AI Act is interpreted in practice. The Apply AI Summit in Brussels — convened under the European Commission’s Digital Strategy umbrella — will bring together policymakers, regulators, and industry leaders to translate the Act’s text into operational guidance. The IAPP AI Governance Global Europe conference will focus specifically on compliance frameworks for high-risk AI systems, including those used in political advertising, voter targeting, and election administration. Meanwhile, AI Summit London (June 10–11) and GITEX AI Europe in Berlin (June 30–July 1) round out a summer conference circuit that campaign technology vendors operating across borders cannot afford to skip. The first binding obligations of the EU AI Act took effect in February 2026, and companies deploying high-risk systems face compliance deadlines by August. For political technology firms, this means the rules governing AI use in European elections are being negotiated right now — in conference rooms, not courtrooms.

Should the US Have Paused or Escalated in the Strait of Hormuz?

“The Pause Was Necessary — Diplomacy Requires Space”

Proponents of Trump’s decision to pause Project Freedom argue that military escalation in a contested waterway risked triggering a wider war at sea — one that could spike global oil prices, devastate economies, and endanger the 23,000 stranded sailors the operation claimed to protect. Pakistan and other regional actors reportedly urged restraint. With Iran’s foreign minister heading to Beijing, the pause creates a diplomatic window that did not exist 48 hours ago. The U.S. blockade of Iranian ports remains in place as leverage, and a negotiated reopening of the strait — rather than a military one — would be more durable and less costly in lives and treasure.

Sources: NPR · Al Jazeera

“Pausing After One Day Projects Weakness, Not Strength”

Critics, including hawkish Republicans and several allied navies, argue that launching a major military operation and pulling it back within 24 hours is the worst of both worlds: it signals that Iran can fire on American forces and suffer no escalation, while stranded sailors remain trapped. Secretary Rubio had just hours earlier described the situation as life-or-death. The about-face hands Tehran a propaganda victory — proof that its blockade works — and makes future American military commitments in the region less credible. If no deal materialises, resuming the operation will be harder, not easier, because Iran will have had time to fortify its naval positions.

Convos — AI-Powered Voter Persuasion via Text Message

Convos is an AI chatbot platform that allows campaigns to deploy personalised, conversational voter outreach at scale via SMS. The system ingests a candidate’s website, past remarks, and approved talking points to create a custom AI agent that engages voters in real-time text conversations. Unlike robocalls or blast texts, each Convos interaction is unique — the AI adapts its arguments to the voter’s responses, creating what the company calls ‘a modern type of grassroots organising.’

In Wisconsin’s April 2026 Supreme Court race, the Defend Our Courts PAC deployed Convos to contact more than 1.1 million voters identified as undecided. Over 10,000 engaged in extended conversations with the chatbot, which advocated for the Democratic-aligned candidate Susan Crawford — who won. Total cost: $84,000. The platform does not disclose its AI nature unless explicitly asked. UW-Madison professor Dietram Scheufele warns this represents ‘uncharted territory’ — the ability to spread tailored persuasion at scale and in real time was previously impossible.

> takeaway: Convos demonstrates that AI persuasion tools have crossed from research into live deployment. At $84K for 1.1M contacts, the cost-per-conversation undercuts traditional canvassing by orders of magnitude. Campaigns without an AI chatbot strategy in 2026 may be bringing a clipboard to a machine-learning fight.

Sources: Wisconsin Public Radio · Deseret News · Convos.ai
