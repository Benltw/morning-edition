Testing Theories of Political Persuasion Using AI

Argyle, Busby, Gubler, Lyman, Olcott, Pond & Wingate -- PNAS, 122(18), 2025

Researchers at Brigham Young University used large language models to isolate and experimentally test two mechanisms that political persuasion theory has long hypothesized as powerful: message customization through microtargeting and cognitive elaboration through interactive dialogue. Across two original studies involving controversial U.S. political issues, the team found that neither mechanism added meaningful persuasive power beyond a single generic AI-generated message. A well-crafted one-shot statement moved attitudes about as effectively as a personalized, interactive exchange -- challenging a core assumption of modern digital campaigning.

More striking still, the researchers found that even where AI messages did moderate people's policy attitudes, those shifts failed to translate into increased democratic reciprocity -- the willingness to grant political opponents legitimacy and fair treatment. Attitude moderation and democratic tolerance appear to be decoupled processes, meaning that a campaign could successfully move a voter's position on an issue without reducing affective polarization or hostility toward the opposing side.

The study demonstrates that LLMs can serve as efficient experimental infrastructure for testing persuasion theories at scale, overcoming logistical barriers that have constrained the field for decades. But the substantive finding is a caution: the most sophisticated AI persuasion techniques may not outperform simpler approaches, and moving opinions does not necessarily heal democratic culture.

Practitioner implication: Campaigns investing in AI-powered microtargeting and interactive chatbot persuasion should temper expectations -- simpler, cheaper generic messaging may produce equivalent attitude shifts, and neither approach appears to reduce the partisan hostility that corrodes democratic norms.
Sources: PNAS 122(18), 2025 (doi:10.1073/pnas.2412815122); PsyPost, 19 Mar 2026

Trump Scraps AI Executive Order: "I Think It Gets in the Way"

Hours before AI executives were due at the White House for a signing ceremony, President Trump pulled his administration's much-anticipated executive order on artificial intelligence. The order would have established a voluntary framework for AI companies to share advanced models with federal agencies for up to 90 days of security review before public release. It was split into two sections -- one on cybersecurity, one on frontier model testing -- and represented months of negotiation between the White House, OpenAI, and Anthropic.

Trump told reporters the order could have been a "blocker" and that AI is "causing tremendous good." His AI adviser David Sacks also opposed the measure. The postponement leaves the United States without any federal framework -- voluntary or mandatory -- for evaluating frontier AI models before deployment. For advocates of AI safety regulation, the reversal was a gut punch: the White House had been the last plausible vehicle for executive-branch action, and congressional legislation remains stalled.

The accelerationist wing of the administration has, for now, prevailed. But the underlying tension between the intelligence community's demand for pre-release access and industry's resistance to any review process has not been resolved -- it has merely been deferred.

Trump's Revenge Tour Rattles the GOP Majority

President Trump's campaign to primary Republican critics is delivering scalps -- and creating problems he cannot afford. Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana failed to make his primary runoff after Trump endorsed his opponent and attacked him relentlessly. Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky fell to Trump-backed Ed Gallrein in what became the most expensive House primary in American history, with over $32.6 million in ad spending. And this week Trump endorsed Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton over incumbent Senator John Cornyn, adding another senior Republican to the list of allies-turned-enemies.

The victories feed Trump's dominance narrative, but the arithmetic is turning against him. The GOP Senate majority is razor-thin, and each unseated or alienated Republican senator is a potential defection on the reconciliation bill that carries the bulk of Trump's second-term domestic agenda. Cornyn, who chairs the Senate Judiciary Committee, now has little incentive to carry water for a president who endorsed his rival. Cassidy's replacement, whoever emerges from the Louisiana runoff, will be a freshman with no committee seniority and no institutional loyalty beyond fealty to Trump.

The pattern mirrors a structural tension in Trumpism: the movement's need for total loyalty conflicts with the legislative reality that governing requires coalitions, not purges. The revenge tour has demonstrated that Trump can destroy Republican careers. Whether he can build a governing majority from the rubble remains an open question as the reconciliation clock ticks toward a summer recess deadline.

The Redistricting Arms Race: When Algorithms Draw the Maps

Both parties are now deploying AI algorithms that can generate and evaluate millions of hypothetical congressional maps for a given state, fundamentally changing the redistricting fight ahead of the 2026 midterms. The Algorithm-Assisted Redistricting Methodology (ALARM) Project has built tools that draw thousands of maps complying with any set of specified rules, then score them against metrics like compactness, minority representation, and partisan lean.

Texas Republicans triggered the current arms race by implementing a mid-decade redistrict -- five years ahead of the normal census-driven cycle -- to capture as many as five additional GOP seats. North Carolina followed; a panel of federal judges found its new map would have a disparate impact on Black voters but declined to block it for 2026. Democrats retaliated in California and Virginia, where redraws authorized by voters will add up to nine blue seats.

The technology cuts both ways. AI can expose gerrymanders by comparing a proposed map against the statistical universe of all plausible alternatives, revealing when a map is a partisan outlier. But the same tools can also optimize gerrymanders with surgical precision, finding maps that maximize partisan advantage while technically satisfying legal constraints. The question is no longer whether algorithms will shape American representation -- it is who controls them.

Ongoing: Colombia Votes May 31 Under a Shadow of Political Violence

Eight days before Colombia's presidential first round, the campaign trail has become a killing field. Two staffers for right-wing candidate Abelardo de la Espriella were gunned down by motorcycle assailants last week, echoing the assassination of presidential hopeful Miguel Uribe Turbay last year -- the first killing of a Colombian presidential candidate in over three decades. Rebel groups have staged 26 attacks with explosives and drones in the southwestern corridor since Friday. At least three of the 14 candidates on the ballot have received death threats.

The polling picture has not shifted dramatically from this magazine's previous coverage, but the security context has. Ivan Cepeda of Petro's leftist Historic Pact leads at 44.3 percent in the most recent Invamer survey. De la Espriella holds second at 21.5 percent, with Paloma Valencia of the Centro Democratico stalling at around 19.8 percent. No candidate is expected to clear 50 percent, setting up a June 21 runoff. The International Committee of the Red Cross has declared 2025 Colombia's worst humanitarian year in a decade.

The election will determine the fate of FARC peace agreements, Colombia's response to the Venezuelan refugee crisis, and whether South America's third-largest economy continues its experiment with left-wing governance or pivots sharply right -- all under conditions that the Stanford Freeman Spogli Institute has described as elections conducted in the shadow of violence.

Deepfake Political Ads Proliferate -- 28 States Act, Congress Does Not

The National Republican Senatorial Committee's deepfake of Texas Democratic Senate nominee James Talarico -- a fabricated video in which a synthetic Talarico appears to speak directly into the camera for over a minute -- has become the defining artifact of AI-manipulated political advertising in the 2026 cycle. The words "AI GENERATED" appear in small text for approximately three seconds near the start, then shrink to near-invisible fine print for the remainder. It is not an outlier: at least 15 campaign ads featuring AI-generated content have aired since November, and the technology has improved to the point where earlier telltale glitches have been eliminated.

Twenty-eight states have now passed legislation addressing AI in political ads, most focused on disclosure requirements rather than outright bans. But no federal law constrains the practice, leaving a patchwork of largely untested state rules as the only guardrail. House Democrats plan to push comprehensive regulation if they retake the chamber, but that legislation would not take effect until 2027 at the earliest. In the meantime, the 2026 midterms are the first major American election in which deepfake political ads are both technically convincing and strategically deployed at scale.

Research from the World Economic Forum warns that widespread synthetic media represents a destabilizing systemic force capable of disrupting democracies and eroding social cohesion. A recent survey found that 58 percent of U.S. adults expect AI-generated political deception to escalate before ballots are cast in November. Republicans appear to be using the technology more frequently this cycle, though Democratic super PACs have also deployed AI-enhanced content in competitive races.

The regulatory gap is not accidental. The same AI industry super PACs flooding midterm races with spending -- Leading the Future, Think Big, American Mission -- have lobbied aggressively against mandatory disclosure rules at both the state and federal level. The result is a political advertising environment in which the technology to fabricate a candidate's words and appearance is freely available, the regulatory framework to police it is fragmented and weak, and the financial incentives all point toward escalation.

64% vs. 49%: The GOP-Democrat AI Adoption Gap in Political Consulting

The American Association of Political Consultants' latest survey has quantified a divide that operatives on both sides have felt for months: 64 percent of Republican political consultants report using AI tools daily in their work, compared with 49 percent of Democrats. More than eight in ten consultants across both parties use AI at least a few times a week, but the intensity and enthusiasm gap favors the right.

The disparity reflects different institutional cultures. GOP strategist Eric Wilson, an AI evangelist, has argued publicly that Republicans have a professional advantage because they are willing to push the envelope and take risks with new technology. The Republican ecosystem has embraced tools like Aaru, which replicates pools of voters as AI agents that can be polled on their views before real voters are contacted. Democrats, by contrast, have been more cautious -- the DNC solicited proposals for a next-generation voter database but has moved slowly on AI integration, and some Democratic campaigns have internally banned ChatGPT over data security concerns.

The sentiment trajectory is also shifting. Forty-three percent of consultants now say AI will have a positive impact on the future of campaigns, up from 33 percent in 2025. But 67 percent still believe additional government regulation is needed, with sharp partisan disagreement on how far those rules should go. The gap between enthusiasm in practice and anxiety in principle defines the profession's uneasy relationship with the technology reshaping it.

Sources: Campaigns & Elections, 2026; Axios, 14 Apr 2026; Semafor, 8 Apr 2026

Agentic AI Enters State Government: Alaska, Indiana, Virginia Lead

Three states are pioneering agentic AI -- autonomous systems that execute multistep tasks without human approval at each step -- in public-sector operations. Alaska's Department of Administration has published a request for information outlining plans to add agentic capabilities to its state mobile app, including proactive notifications, dynamic form-filling, and autonomous data exchange across agencies. Indiana has deployed bilingual AI agents that provide 24/7 support for residents, offering one of the first working solutions to the human-in-the-loop problem in government services.

Virginia's experiment is more fraught. Former Governor Glenn Youngkin launched a first-of-its-kind pilot using agentic AI to scan all state regulations, spot redundancies, and suggest streamlined language -- dubbing it the "Virginia Model" of regulatory modernization. But Youngkin's executive order is no longer hosted on the state's website, and Governor Abigail Spanberger's administration has not indicated whether the program will continue. The transition illustrates the fragility of AI governance initiatives that depend on executive orders rather than legislation.

A World Economic Forum readiness framework published this year warns that agentic AI in government requires new accountability structures that most agencies have not yet built. Instances of autonomous AI agents operating without human approval remain rare in state government, but the trajectory is clear: from Alaska's digital portal to Indiana's constituent services, the technology is moving from concept papers to production systems faster than governance frameworks can keep pace.

The MIT Blueprint: Using AI to Strengthen Democracy, Not Break It

MIT Technology Review published a three-layer framework this month for ensuring that AI reinforces democratic institutions rather than corroding them. The authors -- Andrew Sorota and Josh Hendler, who lead work on AI and democracy at the Office of Eric Schmidt -- argue that AI is becoming the primary interface through which citizens form beliefs and participate in self-governance, and that this shift could either strain already fragile institutions or help address problems like polarization and declining civic engagement.

The informational layer calls on AI companies to ensure model outputs are truthful and to build on early findings that AI can reduce polarization. The authors cite a field evaluation on X in which users across the political spectrum rated AI-written fact-check notes as more helpful than human-written ones -- a finding they describe as potentially revolutionary. The agentic layer demands that AI agents never develop agendas of their own or misrepresent their users' views, and should not shield users from uncomfortable information. The institutional layer points to states and localities already using AI-mediated platforms for democratic deliberation at scale.

The sharpest warning is structural: a public sphere in which everyone has a personalized agent attuned to their existing views is, in aggregate, not a public sphere at all. It is a collection of private worlds, each internally coherent but collectively inhospitable to the shared deliberation that democracy requires. The blueprint rejects both techno-optimism and techno-pessimism in favor of a design agenda -- one that treats the democratic implications of AI as engineering problems with institutional solutions.

Sources: MIT Technology Review, 5 May 2026; TechPolicy.Press, 2026

$44 Million and a Barber Shop: PTP's Midterm Ground Game

The Progressive Turnout Project is deploying $44.1 million across five voter contact initiatives ahead of November, making it one of the largest single ground-game investments of the 2026 cycle. The breakdown: $35 million on door knocking, $4 million on a program dedicated to electing pro-democracy candidates, $2.5 million on training campaign fellows in canvassing technique, $2.1 million on postcards to inconsistent Democratic voters, and $500,000 on voter engagement through beauty salons and barber shops.

The barber shop program reflects a broader Democratic bet that unconventional venues can reach voters who have tuned out traditional campaign contact. PTP's president, Alex Morgan, has argued that major committees chronically underinvest in turnout operations and make late investments when the work is most effective. PTP's approach is to spend early and sustain contact through the cycle, rather than flooding the zone in the final weeks.

The operation's technology backbone is also evolving. PTP has taken a 50 percent stake in TouchStone, a canvassing software startup, steering away from NGP VAN's MiniVAN app that has dominated Democratic field operations for over a decade. The partnership ran a pilot in Virginia last fall and is scaling to California, Georgia, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania. The investment signals that even the party's largest ground-game organizations are losing confidence in the legacy technology infrastructure.

Should AI-Generated Political Ads Be Banned?

The Case for a Ban

Consumer advocates and election integrity organizations argue that AI-generated political ads -- particularly deepfakes depicting candidates saying things they never said -- represent an existential threat to informed democratic choice. Disclosure requirements are insufficient because labels are routinely minimized to near-invisibility, as the NRSC's Talarico ad demonstrated. Voters cannot be expected to evaluate the authenticity of every piece of political media they encounter.

Twenty-eight states have passed legislation, but the patchwork is untested and unenforceable across platforms that operate nationally. A federal ban on synthetic depictions of candidates without their consent would establish a clear, enforceable baseline. Without it, the 2028 presidential election will be the first in which fabricated candidate statements are indistinguishable from real ones.

Sources: WEF, Mar 2026; FakeOut.io, 2026; CNN, 13 Mar 2026

The Case Against a Ban

Free speech advocates and technologists warn that banning AI-generated political content would create a censorship framework that could be weaponized against legitimate political expression. Satire, parody, and counterfactual political commentary have always been protected speech. Drawing a legal line between a deepfake attack ad and an AI-assisted satirical video is technically and constitutionally fraught.

The better approach is transparency: mandatory, prominent, standardized disclosure labels enforced at the platform level, combined with rapid-response verification infrastructure. A ban would also be technologically unenforceable -- detection tools lag behind generation tools, and prohibition would simply push production offshore. The goal should be an informed electorate, not a censored one.

Sources: American Prospect, 17 Apr 2026; NCSL, 2026; Semafor, Apr 2026
